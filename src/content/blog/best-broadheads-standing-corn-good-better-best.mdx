The best broadhead for bowhunting standing corn isn't a secret brand. It's the head you can shoot accurately, that leaves a blood trail you can follow through rows, and that doesn't cost more than your bow. This guide gives you three Good/Better/Best picks, and it's honest about one thing up front: we found no study on broadheads and corn stalks, so we'll mark where we're reasoning from the field instead of the research.

Timing matters here. Standing corn only works until the combine shows up. USDA's report for the week ending Oct. 25 had 60 percent of the national corn crop harvested, behind the 66 percent average. Ohio stood at 35 percent against a 46 percent average, and Michigan at 28 percent against 43. Fields are coming down, but a lot of corn is still up.

If you want the stand, wind, and shot-lane side of this, start with our [standing corn bowhunting guide](/blog/bowhunting-standing-corn/). This one is about what's on the end of the arrow.

## What Standing Corn Changes About Broadhead Choice

This section is our reading of the situation, not a study result.

- **A deer that's hit disappears.** In open ground you watch it run. In standing corn it's gone in two rows. So the real question is what happens after the shot: how easy is the blood trail, and does the arrow pass through?
- **Shots are close.** Standing corn hides deer until they're near. That favors a head that flies true at short range and doesn't need a long flight to "open up."
- **Clear lanes matter more than the head.** Leaves and stalks in the flight path are a bigger risk than any broadhead feature. Our [shot-lane advice](/blog/bowhunting-standing-corn/) covers how to clear a lane with the landowner's permission.

## Fixed vs. Mechanical: What One Study Found

One study we found compared the two types on real deer. It's a 2014 paper in the *Journal of the Southeastern Association of Fish and Wildlife Agencies* by Pedersen, Berry, and Bossart. They went back through hunter data sheets from a managed bowhunt at a Maryland naval facility, covering 1989 to 2012.

Of 1,296 deer hit, 1,083 were recovered. Recovery meant finding the deer within 24 hours.

- **Fixed blade:** 82.0 percent recovered (874 of 1,066).
- **Mechanical:** 90.9 percent recovered (209 of 230).

The gap showed up with compound bows (82.0 vs. 88.8 percent) and crossbows (81.5 vs. 95.7 percent). The authors suggested the wider cut of a mechanical head seemed to make up for any worries about penetration.

**The fine print:** This wasn't a controlled test. Hunters picked their own gear, some used both types, and the crossbow sample was small. It was one managed hunt in Maryland, not corn country. It didn't measure penetration or blood trails. So it's a nudge, not a verdict.

## How to Pick

- **Good** if you want a simple fixed head with nothing to deploy and your lane is clean.
- **Better** if you want a big hole at a fair price, and the recovery numbers we found lean toward mechanical.
- **Best** if you want the head with the strongest overall reputation and you're willing to pay for it.

Whatever you buy, shoot it before you hunt with it. Group it against your field points at the distance you'll actually take a shot. A head that doesn't fly true isn't a good head, however it's rated.

## FAQ: Broadheads for Standing Corn

### Are mechanical broadheads better than fixed for standing corn?

One Maryland study found higher recovery with mechanical heads: 90.9 percent against 82.0 percent for fixed. It wasn't a controlled test and wasn't done in corn, so treat it as a nudge. Shot placement and a clear lane matter more than the head.

### Does corn affect broadhead flight?

We didn't find a study on it. What we can say is that leaves and stalks in the flight path are a risk for any arrow. Clear a lane, with the landowner's permission, so the arrow has nothing to touch.

### How long will standing corn be available?

Until it's harvested. USDA's report for the week ending Oct. 25 had 60 percent of the national corn crop harvested, behind the 66 percent average. Ohio was at 35 percent and Michigan at 28, both behind their averages. Check your own fields. ## The Bottom Line

In standing corn, the shot is close and the deer disappears fast. Pick a head you can shoot well, lean toward a wide cut, and spend your effort on a clean lane. The corn won't be there long, and once it's cut, the hunt moves to the edges. Our [cut cornfield guide](/blog/reading-a-cut-cornfield/) picks up from there.
