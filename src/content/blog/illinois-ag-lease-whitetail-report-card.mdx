## Before You Lease Deer Hunting Land in Illinois: Permits, the Two-Buck Cap, and Baiting

Before the grades, a few Illinois rules that change what a lease is worth.

- **Two antlered deer a year, all seasons combined.** Illinois has no set limit on total deer. The bag limit is one deer per permit, but you can't take more than two antlered deer in a year. The special CWD season in select counties is the one exception.
- **Permits come from a lottery, and a lease doesn't get you around it.** Landowner permits are free, but they're for owners and for resident tenants who lease at least 40 acres of commercial farmland. In listed CWD counties the bar drops to 20 acres. IDNR says a hunting-rights lease doesn't qualify anyone for them. So the farmer who owns your ground may hold free permits for his own use. You'll be in the lottery like everybody else. Our [Illinois season dates page](/illinois-deer-season-dates/) has the lottery rounds and costs.
- **Baiting is illegal, but food plots and crops are fine.** Illinois law bars taking deer by use or aid of bait. It counts an area as baited while bait is there and for 10 days after it's gone. Food, salt, and minerals all count as bait. Food plots, standing crops, and normal farming are excepted, so a planted plot is fine and a pile of corn isn't.
- **Rifles are legal statewide in counties with a firearm season.** Illinois allows single-shot centerfire rifles that meet its caliber and energy rules. IDNR's 2026-27 card puts Cook, DuPage, Lake, and the part of Kane County east of Route 47 on an archery-only schedule. Our [straight-wall cartridge guide](/blog/straight-wall-cartridge-state-regulations/) and the season dates page have the details.

**Why this belongs on a lease report card:** The permit rule is the one that surprises people. Paying for hunting rights gets you ground, but it doesn't get you a tag.

## Buck Quality: B

**How we graded it:** Boone and Crockett's ranking of states by whitetail entries. Top three is an A, fourth through seventh is a B, eighth through twelfth is a C, and anything lower is a D.

Illinois ranks fifth. Boone and Crockett's latest ranking, covering entries from January 1, 2022 to December 31, 2024, gives Illinois 105 entries, two behind Iowa's 107. Wisconsin leads with 135, then Indiana with 126 and Ohio with 125. Kentucky is sixth with 98.

Illinois used to sit higher. It was second in the all-time books through 2019. In the 2020 to 2022 window it ranked sixth with 77 entries, so the 105 is a real climb from the last ranking. Boone and Crockett credits river-bottom hardwoods along the Illinois and Mississippi valleys for the big non-typicals, and says the state is no longer just producing a few outliers.

**The fine print:** Record-book entries count the very top of the herd, not the average buck on your lease. A statewide rank says nothing about your county. If you want to know why ag-country bucks can look older than they are, see [our Field Note on that](/blog/why-ag-country-bucks-look-older-than-they-are/).

## Deer Numbers: A

**How we graded it:** The latest season's harvest against the three-year average. Above average and in the top ten seasons ever is an A. Within 10 percent below average is a B. Ten to 25 percent below is a C. Anything worse is a D. A season that's above average but not top ten lands in the B range.

IDNR's final report counts 174,006 deer across all seasons in 2025-26. The three seasons before it were 157,854 in 2022-23, 160,313 in 2023-24, and 171,322 in 2024-25, an average of about 163,200. That puts 2025-26 about 7 percent above average.

It's also the tenth-highest season on record. IDNR's historical table shows nine seasons that beat it, all between 2004 and 2012. The next one up, 2012, was about 4 percent higher. That's a thin margin, but it clears the bar.

Illinois' record is 201,209 deer in 2005, and 2025-26 sits about 14 percent below it. Hunters took 81,316 deer in firearm season and 73,785 in archery. IDNR issued 581,765 permits, up about 3 percent from 2024.

**The fine print:** Our total includes the late-winter and CWD seasons, which are management hunts in select counties. Those two took about 11,500 deer this year. Last year's combined number was about 7,800, and that figure was preliminary. So most of the year-over-year gain came from those hunts, not from the regular seasons. Reported harvest is also a floor, not a count of every deer on the ground, and the statewide number blends very different parts of the state.

## Access: D

**How we graded it:** Public land as a share of the state's total land. Twenty percent or more is an A, 10 to 19 percent is a B, 5 to 9 percent is a C, and under 5 percent is a D.

IDNR's own magazine says about 97 percent of Illinois is privately owned. That leaves roughly 3 percent public, which is a D. IDNR manages close to half a million acres of hunting sites. The Shawnee National Forest, the state's largest public landowner, adds somewhere around 280,000 to 290,000 acres in the far south, depending on the source.

IDNR also runs a program that leases private ground for public hunting, the Illinois Recreational Access Program. It has enrolled more than 21,000 acres in dozens of counties, which is a small slice next to the private acres.

**The fine print:** IDNR's own articles don't agree on the exact share. Some say 97 percent private, others 96 or 95. We used 97 because it fits the acreage count, since IDNR's hunting sites alone come to about 1.3 percent of the state. Even at 95 percent private, Illinois would only reach the bottom of the C range.

This is also a statewide number, and the Shawnee acres are in the far south, a long way from corn and bean country. In most of Illinois farm country, the ground you can hunt is ground somebody owns.

## Herd Health: C-plus

**How we graded it:** CWD and EHD results. An A is no wild CWD and no big EHD losses. A B means CWD is present but confined to a few counties and shows up in about 1 percent or less of tested deer, with a minus for a regional disease hit. A C means CWD is widespread or a region loses deer to EHD year after year. A D is both.

**CWD:** Between July 1, 2025 and June 30, 2026, IDNR tested 13,225 usable samples and found 662 positives, about 5 percent. Positives have now turned up in 28 of Illinois' 102 counties, and three were new this year: Effingham, Scott, and Stark. Since the disease was found, IDNR has logged about 3,400 positives in roughly 199,000 tests, about 1.7 percent. Among adult deer taken by hunters in the 28-county area, 10.4 percent tested positive. Adult bucks ran 12.5 percent and adult does 7.7 percent.

Most early cases were in northern Illinois. New detections are showing up farther south and west now, in counties like Adams, Effingham, and Scott. That's why this isn't a B. Only about a quarter of the counties have it, so it isn't a full C either.

**EHD:** IDNR's most recent write-up covers 2024. Hunters reported 626 suspected EHD deaths from 57 counties, the highest count in a decade and fourth behind 2007, 2012, and 2013. IDNR said the effect on the herd, statewide and by county, was minimal. We couldn't find an official 2025 count.

**The fine print:** Testing isn't random. Most samples come from CWD counties, so the 5 percent isn't the rate across the whole state. IDNR's report also has small internal mismatches in its totals, which is why we rounded. For the basics, read [our CWD explainer](/blog/chronic-wasting-disease-explained/) and check IDNR's CWD map for the county you want.

## Lease Market and Law: D

**How we graded it:** Two halves. One is Illinois' landowner liability law, and whether it still protects a landowner who accepts a lease payment. The other is cropland cost against the national average, since land cost pushes lease prices up. Our grade averages the two halves. On cropland, within about 15 percent of the national average is an A, 15 to 30 percent over is a B, 30 to 50 percent over is a C, and more than 50 percent over is a D.

**The law protects free access (law half: C).** Illinois' recreational use law is the Recreational Use of Land and Water Areas Act, 745 ILCS 65. It says a landowner who lets people use land for recreation, which includes hunting, owes no duty to keep it safe. The landowner also isn't responsible for injuries caused by other users. But the law ties that protection to access "without charge," and it doesn't limit liability when the owner charges. Willful and wanton failure to guard against or warn of a known danger is never covered.

The law defines a charge as "an admission fee for permission to go upon the land." Sharing game or fish doesn't count as one. The law also carves out one kind of payment: money a landowner gets for leasing land to the state or a local government. A hunter's lease check isn't covered by that carve-out.

The text doesn't mention hunting lease payments by name. A lease check is a fee for permission to go on the land, so we read it as a charge, the same way we read Indiana, Iowa, and Minnesota. That means a paying hunter may be owed a higher duty of care. The state's own Land Access Permission Card works the same way. It's a hunter-and-landowner agreement that waives injury claims, but it's void if money changes hands. A paid lease needs its own written release. That's our reading of the statute, not legal advice, and we didn't review court cases. Check the current text before you sign anything.

**Cropland is nearly Iowa-expensive (cropland half: D).** USDA's 2026 land values report puts Illinois cropland at $10,200 per acre, up 3.6 percent from 2025. The U.S. average is $6,020, so Illinois runs about 69 percent higher. For comparison, Iowa is at $10,700, Ohio $10,100, Indiana $8,600, Wisconsin $7,600, Minnesota $7,300, and Michigan $6,600. That's a lot of money tied up in the dirt, so expect owners to price hunting rights with the crop in mind.

A C for the law and a D for cropland average to 1.5, which we round down to a D, the way we've rounded the other half-points in this series.

**The fine print:** We couldn't find an official Illinois per-acre hunting lease price. Our [Corn Belt lease cost piece](/blog/cost-of-leasing-ag-ground/) found some hot Illinois and Iowa counties reportedly bidding well past the usual range. Get anything you sign reviewed by a lawyer, and see [what to look for when leasing hunting land](/blog/leasing-hunting-property-what-to-look-for/).

## What the Grades Mean If You Lease Deer Ground in Illinois

- **Don't count on the lease for tags.** The landlord's free permits are for his own use, and your lease doesn't qualify you for any. Know the lottery rounds and the county's permit quota before you pay.
- **Plan on private ground.** Only about 3 percent of the state is public, so most of your options are farms somebody owns.
- **Ask about CWD in the county.** Find out if it's been detected, and plan to test any deer you take. IDNR runs check stations in CWD counties during firearm season.
- **Get the paperwork.** A written lease, a signed release, and liability insurance matter more here, since payment likely ends the statute's shield.
- **Don't pay for the record book alone.** Illinois' big bucks are real, but the state sits fifth now, and the top end comes from river-bottom country. Ask what's actually come off the farm you're looking at.
- **Check the dates and the license costs.** Our [Illinois deer season dates page](/illinois-deer-season-dates/) has this year's seasons, and our [7-state license guide](/blog/deer-hunting-license-guide-seven-state/) has the costs.

## FAQ: Illinois Deer Hunting Leases

### Is Illinois a good state to lease deer ground?

For deer numbers and bucks, yes. Illinois had its tenth-largest harvest on record in 2025-26 and ranks fifth in Boone and Crockett's 2022 to 2024 entries. The catch is access and cost. About 97 percent of the state is private, cropland runs $10,200 per acre, and CWD has been found in 28 counties.

### How much does it cost to lease hunting land in Illinois?

We couldn't find an official Illinois per-acre lease price. Our Corn Belt lease cost piece found some hot Illinois and Iowa counties reportedly bidding well above the usual range. USDA puts Illinois cropland at $10,200 per acre, about 69 percent above the national average, and land cost tends to pull lease prices up.

### Is a landowner protected from liability if they lease land to hunters in Illinois?

Probably not fully. The Recreational Use of Land and Water Areas Act protects landowners who allow use without charge, and it doesn't limit liability when the owner charges. The law doesn't name hunting lease payments, but we read a lease fee as a charge. A written lease, a signed release, and insurance are the usual safeguards. Talk to a lawyer before signing.

### Does a hunting lease get me landowner permits in Illinois?

No. IDNR says a hunting-rights lease doesn't qualify anyone for landowner or tenant permits. Those go to landowners and to resident tenants who lease at least 40 acres of commercial farmland. A hunter who leases hunting rights applies through the regular permit lottery. Illinois also caps hunters at two antlered deer a year across all seasons.

## The Bottom Line

Illinois gets a C-plus (2.3). That's behind Indiana's C-plus (2.5) and ahead of Iowa's C (2.0). It ties Ohio for the best Deer Numbers grade in the series, and it shares the lowest Lease Market grade with Iowa.

Here's how all seven landed: Minnesota B (3.0), Wisconsin B-minus (2.9), Michigan B-minus (2.9), Ohio B-minus (2.8), Indiana C-plus (2.5), Illinois C-plus (2.3), and Iowa C (2.0). The pattern is clear. The states with the best bucks are the states where ground is hardest to get and most expensive to hold.

If you lease in Illinois, ask about the CWD history before the scouting. Get the release and the insurance in writing, and don't pay for tags a lease can't give you.
