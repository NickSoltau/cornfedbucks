**Not every soybean field in Indiana is running on the same clock.** Some ground gets planted to beans in May and comes off in September like normal. Other ground grows a wheat crop first, then gets replanted to beans in July — and that field is still green and standing weeks after its neighbor's been cut. If you're reading cut fields to time your October sits, that second calendar is worth knowing before it catches you off guard.

## Indiana's Two Soybean Calendars

Double-cropping means planting soybeans right behind a wheat harvest, in the same field, in the same year. [Purdue Extension](https://www.purdue.edu/newsroom/archive/releases/2013/Q3/delay-in-wheat-harvest-cuts-time-to-plant-double-crop-soybeans.html) puts the planting deadline at roughly July 15–25 depending on location — soybeans need about 90 days from emergence to maturity, and that clock has to finish before the first frost.

A full-season bean field planted in May and a double-crop field planted in July are running roughly a month apart the whole way through. By the time full-season beans are cut, a double-crop field two fence lines over can still be green — a food source most hunters walk right past because "the beans are still up" reads as *not ready*, not as *running on a different calendar.*

## Where Double-Crop Ground Concentrates

Double-cropping isn't spread evenly across the state — it follows where wheat gets grown and where the growing season leaves enough runway for a second crop to mature.

- **Southern Indiana** is the state's core double-crop belt. A longer fall before frost gives beans planted in July enough time to finish.
- **Central Indiana** sits in a transition zone — some ground double-crops, some doesn't, depending on the individual farm's wheat rotation and how early frost tends to hit that specific spot.
- **Northern Indiana** plants noticeably less wheat than the southern third of the state (per USDA/NASS county data) and runs a shorter frost-free window on top of that — full-season beans dominate there, and double-cropping is the exception, not the rule.

If you hunt leased ag ground in the southern third of the state, checking whether a given field was in wheat this spring is worth doing before you write it off as "already cut" in early October.

## Don't Trust Last Year's Calendar

Wheat harvest timing moves the whole double-crop calendar with it — same lesson as [Ohio's corn harvest split](/blog/ohio-two-stage-corn-harvest-deer-hunting) and [Iowa's soybean harvest timeline](/blog/iowa-soybean-harvest-timeline): check the current year's numbers, not your memory.

As of late May 2026, [Purdue Extension small grains specialist Shaun Casteel reported](https://www.wwbl.com/2026/05/27/indiana-winter-wheat-outpaces-the-nation-opening-doors-for-double-crop-soybeans/) that Indiana's winter wheat crop was running 7 to 10 days ahead of normal, with 73% rated good to excellent statewide. An earlier wheat harvest opens the door to earlier double-crop bean planting — which, if the pattern holds through summer, could mean this year's double-crop fields mature and get cut earlier than the mid-to-late October window a normal year runs on. Check [USDA/NASS Indiana Crop Progress reports](https://www.nass.usda.gov/Statistics_by_State/Indiana/Publications/Crop_Progress_&_Condition/) for the current numbers before you plan around it — a week-to-ten-day head start in May doesn't guarantee the same gap holds all the way to October.

## Building Your Early-October Sits Around It

- **Identify the double-crop fields near your stands before season, not during it.** A field that was in wheat this spring and beans by July is running the later calendar — worth a mental flag before you write it off as "already cut" in early October.
- **Don't assume a green bean field means "not food yet."** [The day a bean field gets cut, deer start moving on it](/blog/reading-a-cut-soybean-field) — but a double-crop field cut in late October means that shift happens later than the full-season fields around it, not that it isn't coming.
- **Watch the gap between neighboring fields.** When a full-season field goes from green to cut, deer using it often shift into cover or onto nearby food. A still-green double-crop field close by can become that landing spot without you noticing the field even existed.
- **Recheck wind before you commit a stand to a double-crop edge.** [A wind chart built in September](/blog/building-a-preseason-wind-chart) may not hold once a late-cut field changes from cover to open ground in late October — the shift just happens later than it does on full-season ground.

## FAQ: Indiana Double-Crop Soybeans and Deer Hunting

### What is a double-crop soybean field?

It's a soybean field planted immediately after a wheat harvest in the same year and same field, typically in mid-to-late July in Indiana. Because it's planted a couple months later than a full-season bean field, it also matures and gets harvested later — usually mid-to-late October instead of September.

### Where in Indiana is double-cropping most common?

Southern Indiana is the state's core double-crop belt, where a longer frost-free fall gives July-planted beans enough time to mature. Central Indiana is a transition zone, and northern Indiana grows comparatively little wheat, so full-season beans dominate there.

### Why does this matter for early-October deer hunting?

A double-crop field can still be green and standing in early October while full-season fields nearby are already cut. Hunters who assume "beans are beans" can walk past a food source that's simply running a month behind, or miss the later shift into cover when that field finally does come down.

### How do I check this year's harvest timing instead of guessing?

USDA/NASS publishes a weekly Indiana Crop Progress and Condition report during the season, and Purdue Extension covers wheat and double-crop planting conditions each spring. Both are more reliable than assuming this year matches last year.

## The Bottom Line

Indiana doesn't run one soybean calendar — it runs two, a month apart, and the gap concentrates in the southern third of the state where wheat-then-beans is common. A field that looks "not ready" in early October might just be on the later schedule, not behind on food value. Know which fields near your stands were in wheat this spring, check the current year's Crop Progress numbers instead of assuming, and time the bedding-shift window to the calendar that field is actually on.
