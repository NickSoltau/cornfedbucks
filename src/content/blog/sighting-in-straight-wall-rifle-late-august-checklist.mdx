Late August is the window most ag-land hunters waste on **sighting in their straight-wall rifle**. It's hot, deer season feels far away, and it's easy to tell yourself there's plenty of time. There isn't.

Once combines start rolling in September and October, your evenings fill up with scouting and stand prep. That range trip you keep meaning to take gets pushed back until the week before opener — rushed, with no time left to fix a real problem if you find one.

Right now, while the corn's still standing tall, you've got the time and the daylight to do this the right way. Here's the checklist.

## Before You Sight In: Check the Rifle First

I'll admit something first: I've burned through most of a box of ammo chasing a "bad zero" that wasn't a zero problem at all.

My scope mount had worked loose from a season of truck rides, and I didn't think to check it until after I'd already dialed my turret back and forth a dozen times, confused and annoyed. That's not cheap ammo to waste on a mistake you can catch in thirty seconds with a screwdriver.

So before you fire a single shot:

- **Check your action screws.** If you've got a wrench that reads inch-pounds, torque them to spec. A loose stock will shoot sloppy groups no matter how careful you are with the turret.
- **Check your scope ring and base screws too.** A scope that's shifted even a little in the rings will move your point of impact — and it'll look exactly like a zero problem when it isn't.
- **Make sure you're shooting the same ammo your zero is based on.** [Straight-wall cartridges](/blog/how-to-choose-straight-wall-cartridge) drop fast past 150 yards. Switching bullet weight, brand, or even just a new lot of the same factory load can move your point of impact more than you'd think.

Skip this step, and you can spend an entire range session "fixing" a problem that was never in your optic to begin with.

## Let the Standing Corn Do Some Work for You

Here's the part that's specific to this time of year: while the corn's still up, it's actually useful for your range day, not just cover for deer season later on.

Corn rows run about 30 inches apart, pretty consistently. Pace off a few rows next to your range and you've got a rough, repeatable way to mark distances without needing a rangefinder in your hand every time. It's a good habit anyway — the same kind of field-reading you'll lean on once the corn's cut and you're picking apart [terrain funnels in row-crop country](/blog/terrain-funnels-row-crop-country). Knowing what 150 yards actually looks like next to a field edge matters more than knowing what it says on a screen.

Set your range card up at the distances you'll actually shoot at. On this kind of ground, that's usually somewhere between 100 and 250 yards. Don't just zero at 100 and call it done — confirm your drop at 150 and 200, too, and out to 250 if your cartridge and optic can responsibly handle it.

## Make Sure Your Holdover Actually Matches Your Drop

If you're running a BDC dial or reticle, don't just trust the factory numbers. [Those marks are built around a specific ballistic profile](/blog/reticle-choice-bdc-vs-duplex) — a certain bullet, a certain barrel length, a certain velocity. Your setup might not match that exactly, and straight-wall rounds drop fast enough past 150 yards that "close enough" can mean a real miss on a live deer.

So shoot it. Record your actual drop at each distance, and compare it to what the dial or reticle claims. If the numbers don't match, trust your numbers. Every time.

## Shoot From the Spot You'll Actually Be In

A rifle that's dead-on from a bench can print somewhere completely different off a bipod, shooting sticks, or braced in a [ground blind](/blog/ground-blinds) window. Different rest, different cheek weld, different amount of barrel touching something — all of it can nudge your point of impact.

Spend part of your range day shooting from the position you'll actually hunt from, not just off a bench.

If you're also a bowhunter, you already know this in your bones — you wouldn't skip practicing from a treestand and only shoot flat ground all summer. A steep downward angle changes your shot. Same idea here. Confirm your zero from field positions, not just the easy spot at the bench.

## Write It Down

A dope card takes five minutes. Write your load, your real drop at each distance, and today's date. Check it again in October — a summer of storage and a swing in temperature can move a zero more than most people expect.

## The Late-August Sighting-In Checklist

1. Torque your action screws and scope mount screws
2. Confirm you're shooting your actual zero load, not a substitute
3. Pace off real ag-land distances using the standing corn rows
4. Shoot and record your real drop at 100, 150, 200 (and 250 if it applies)
5. Compare your real drop to your BDC dial — trust your numbers over the printed ones
6. Confirm your zero from field positions, not just the bench
7. Write a dope card, and check it again in October
