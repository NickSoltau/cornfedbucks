**Iowa's soybean harvest doesn't happen on one statewide date.** Beans usually come off first in the north and west. South-central Iowa is the region that consistently runs behind — sometimes by two weeks or more at the same point in the season. Knowing where your ground falls in that pattern is the difference between hunting a fresh cut and hunting a field that's already been picked clean.

## Iowa's Soybean Harvest, Region by Region

A cut soybean field is a short window — [we covered why that window closes fast](/blog/reading-a-cut-soybean-field). Statewide, Iowa's soybean harvest usually gets rolling in mid-September and wraps up by early-to-mid November in a normal year. But that statewide average hides a real regional split.

Northwest and north-central Iowa tend to run on pace or a little ahead of the state average. South-central Iowa is the region that shows up behind, year after year, in the USDA's weekly Crop Progress reports.

The exact reason shifts year to year — soil conditions, rainfall timing, equipment access — but the pattern itself is reliable. South-central Iowa shows up as the state's laggard district in year after year of USDA reports, not just once.

## Don't Trust Last Year's Calendar

Harvest timing moves with weather, not the hunting calendar — same lesson as [Ohio's corn harvest split](/blog/ohio-two-stage-corn-harvest-deer-hunting). A wet September can push everything back two weeks. A dry, warm one can pull it forward.

- **Check the current year's numbers, not your memory.** [Iowa's weekly Crop Progress and Condition report](https://www.nass.usda.gov/Statistics_by_State/Iowa/Publications/Crop_Progress_&_Condition/) from USDA/NASS breaks harvest percentage down by district, updated every Monday through the season. It's free and it's the real number for this year, not a guess based on last fall.
- **If you hunt more than one property, check both districts separately.** A guy with ground in northwest Iowa and a lease in south-central Iowa could be looking at beans down on one property and beans still standing on the other, two or three weeks apart, in the same fall.

## Building Your October Stand Sits Around the Timeline

- **Scout for the cut, don't guess at it.** [The day a bean field comes down, deer start moving](/blog/reading-a-cut-soybean-field) — and since the food window on beans closes in days, not weeks, you want to know the cut happened, not assume it happened on schedule.
- **If you're in north or west Iowa, plan your best October sits earlier in the month.** Beans coming off in mid-to-late September means the shuffle into [fence lines](/blog/reading-a-fence-line) and [drainage ditches](/blog/drainage-ditches-as-travel-corridors) is often already underway by the first week of October.
- **If you're in south-central Iowa, hold a stand in reserve.** A later harvest means the bean-field shuffle can land in mid-to-late October instead — right as more hunting pressure hits the area. Don't burn your best setup chasing an early-October pattern that hasn't started yet down there.
- **Re-check wind on any stand tied to a bean field.** [A wind chart built before the beans come down](/blog/building-a-preseason-wind-chart) can read completely different once that field goes from cover to open ground.

## FAQ: Iowa Soybean Harvest and Deer Hunting

### When do soybeans usually get harvested in Iowa?

Statewide, harvest typically starts in mid-September and wraps up by early-to-mid November. Exact dates shift with weather every year, so check the current season's USDA/NASS Crop Progress report instead of relying on a "usual" date.

### Does soybean harvest happen earlier in one part of Iowa than another?

Yes. Northwest and north-central Iowa tend to run on pace or ahead of the state average. South-central Iowa consistently lags behind the rest of the state, sometimes by a couple weeks at the same point in the season — a pattern that's shown up across multiple recent harvests.

### How does this affect stand-sit timing?

In regions where beans come off earlier, the deer shuffle into nearby cover often starts by the first week of October. In regions that run later, like south-central Iowa, that shuffle can land in mid-to-late October instead. Match your best stand sits to when the beans near you are actually likely to come down, not a single statewide date.

### Where can I check current harvest progress for my part of Iowa?

USDA/NASS publishes a weekly Crop Progress and Condition report for Iowa, broken out by district, every Monday during harvest season. It's the most current, most local number available before you plan a sit.

Iowa's soybean harvest doesn't happen on one calendar. North and west Iowa usually leads, south-central Iowa usually trails, and the gap can run two weeks or more. Check the current year's numbers by district, and time your October stand sits to when the beans near you are actually coming down — not to a statewide average that might not apply to your ground.
