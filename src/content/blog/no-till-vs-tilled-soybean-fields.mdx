A no-till soybean field usually holds more waste grain and residue than a tilled one, and deer notice. That difference is why two bean fields cut the same week can pull deer completely differently once the combine's gone.

## It's a Rotation Thing, Not a Farm-Wide Choice

No-till beans are already the norm across most of the Corn Belt — this probably isn't news to you. What matters for hunting is that it's usually one step in a rotation, not a permanent decision. A field that was no-till this year for beans can get tilled next spring to plant corn, and vice versa. So the field you're watching can flip from one to the other year to year, even under the same farmer.

This connects to the same idea in [Reading a Freshly Cut Soybean Field](/blog/reading-a-cut-soybean-field) — the crop tells you part of the story, but it's not the whole story.

## Why Deer Key In on No-Till Soybean Fields

This is the part that actually matters for a stand site. Tillage doesn't just change what a field looks like — it changes what's still there to eat.

A no-till field keeps its residue on the surface: broken cornstalk fragments from last year, scattered bean pods and kernels that shattered during this year's cut, all of it sitting on top of the dirt instead of getting turned under. That surface litter holds onto real food value longer, simply because it's still up where a deer can find it.

Tillage works the opposite way by design. Turning the top few inches of soil over buries whatever was sitting on top — waste grain included — a few inches under where it's a lot harder for a deer's nose or hooves to reach.

That's the mechanism behind something a lot of guys have noticed without knowing why: a picked field can be loaded with deer sign for days, and then go quiet almost overnight once it gets worked. It's not that the deer got spooked off it. The food that was pulling them there just got buried.

## How to Hunt the Difference

- **A no-till field is worth a longer look after the cut.** If the waste grain's still on top, deer have a reason to keep coming back — don't write it off just because it's been a week or two.
- **A tilled field's window closes fast.** Once it's worked, treat it like the food's gone, because it mostly is. Shift your attention to the nearest [fence line](/blog/reading-a-fence-line) or [drainage ditch](/blog/drainage-ditches-as-travel-corridors) instead of waiting on the field to produce again.
- **Watch for the tillage pass itself.** If a field you're watching goes from stubble to worked dirt overnight, that's your cue to move on before you burn a sit on a field that's already lost its draw.
- **Check the ground before you assume.** Loose, worked dirt with little on top means tilled and mostly cleaned out. Firmer ground with stalk fragments and stubble mixed in means no-till — and probably still worth a sit. If your walk-in crosses either kind after a rain, [the right boots](/blog/boots-wet-row-crop-ground) make more difference than you'd think.

Tillage decisions get made by the farmer, not the season, and they can flip year to year on the same ground. The only way to know which one you're looking at is to check — not to assume this year matches last year.
