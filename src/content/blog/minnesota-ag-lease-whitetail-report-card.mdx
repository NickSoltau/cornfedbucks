About a quarter of Minnesota is public land, the bucks are good but not elite, and the herd is climbing back from a rough stretch. That mix is the story of the **Minnesota deer hunting lease** market. The catch is where much of that public ground sits: up north, not in the farm country this series is about. The report card below puts a grade on each piece.

This is the fifth card in our Ag-Lease Whitetail Report Card series. Every state gets graded on the same five categories with the same ruler, so the grades can be compared. ## Minnesota Deer Hunting Lease Report Card: The Grades

**Overall: B.** Plenty of public ground, a harvest that's back above average, and bucks that hold a steady spot in the record book, held back by a CWD problem that keeps growing and a liability law that drops away once a hunter pays.

## Before You Lease in Minnesota: Permit Areas and CWD Zones

Before the grades, a few Minnesota rules that change what a lease is worth.

- **One buck per license year, statewide.** It doesn't matter how many seasons or weapons you use. Tag a buck in archery season and that's your buck.
- **Antlerless deer are set by Deer Permit Area (DPA).** Each DPA is bucks-only, lottery, or has a limit of two, three, or five deer. DNR says bag limits reach three to five deer in the central and southeast parts of the state, while the northeast stays conservative because harvest there follows winter severity.
- **CWD testing rules changed for 2026.** DNR moved to a four-year rotating plan, with the southwest first, then the northwest (2027), northeast (2028), and southeast (2029). This year, testing is mandatory on opening weekend only in a block of 35 DPAs that DNR lists on its CWD page. In the rest of the state, including management zones, it's voluntary. A DPA becomes a management zone once a wild deer tests positive inside it, and 17 were zones for 2025. Hunters can now take a carcass out of a management zone if it goes to a refuse hauler, a landfill, or a meat processor.
- **Rifles are legal in most counties, not all.** Any centerfire rifle .220 caliber or larger with a soft-point or expanding bullet works, and there's no case-shape rule. The old statewide shotgun-only zone was repealed for 2026, but counties can opt out by ordinance. Thirteen did. Eleven are fully shotgun-only: Brown, Cottonwood, Dodge, Faribault, Houston, Jackson, Kandiyohi, Nobles, Pope, Rock, and Watonwan. Clay and Wilkin are shotgun-only in part. DNR says to check with the county where you plan to hunt, because the list can change. Our [Minnesota deer season dates page](/minnesota-deer-season-dates/) has the dates, and our [straight-wall cartridge guide](/blog/straight-wall-cartridge-state-regulations/) covers how Minnesota compares to its neighbors.

**Why this belongs on a lease report card:** Two farms ten miles apart can sit in different DPAs, with different antlerless limits and different CWD rules. Farms on opposite sides of a county line can have different rifle rules. Look up the farm's DPA and county before you pay a dime, especially if you plan to hunt with a rifle.

## Buck Quality: B

**How we graded it:** Boone and Crockett's ranking of states by whitetail entries. Top three is an A, fourth through seventh is a B, eighth through twelfth is a C, and anything lower is a D.

Minnesota is seventh. Boone and Crockett's latest ranking, covering entries from January 1, 2022 to December 31, 2024, puts Wisconsin first with 135, Indiana second with 126, and Ohio third with 125. Iowa has 107, Illinois 105, and Kentucky 98. Minnesota comes next with 70, one entry ahead of Missouri's 69.

That's steady, not a slide. In the 2020 to 2022 window, Minnesota also ranked seventh, with 66 entries. The all-time record is much deeper. Through 2019, Minnesota sat fourth in the all-time books with 1,194 entries, so the three states that passed it since then are simply producing more right now.

**The fine print:** Record-book entries count the very top of the herd, not the average buck on your lease. A statewide rank says nothing about your county. If you want to know why ag-country bucks can look older than they are, see [our Field Note on that](/blog/why-ag-country-bucks-look-older-than-they-are/).

## Deer Numbers: B

**How we graded it:** The latest season's harvest against the three-year average. Above average and in the top ten seasons ever is an A. Within 10 percent below average is a B. Ten to 25 percent below is a C. Anything worse is a D. A season that's above average but not top ten lands in the B range.

DNR's 2025 harvest report counts 186,368 deer, including special hunts and the late CWD season. DNR's early release called it up 9 percent from 2024. The three seasons before it were 172,265 in 2022, 158,678 in 2023, and 170,679 in 2024, an average of about 167,200. That puts 2025 about 11 percent above average.

It's a rebound, not a record. Minnesota's all-time high is 290,525 deer in 2003, and 2025 is about 36 percent below it. Many seasons since 2000 have beaten it. DNR said the mild winter of 2023-24 helped deer start to recover, and the harvest has climbed two years in a row since the 2023 low.

The regions moved differently. Against 2024, DNR reports the northeast up 14 percent, the northwest up 9, the central and southeast up 8, and the southwest up 6. Against the five-year average, the central and southeast are up 11 percent, the best of the four. The northeast is only up 4, and DNR keeps bag limits conservative there.

**The fine print:** Reported harvest is a floor, not a count of every deer on the ground. We used the final report total and not DNR's early number, so our figure differs a little from the January news release. And the statewide number blends the north woods with the farm country. Check the DPA numbers for the county you want.

## Access: A

**How we graded it:** Public land as a share of the state's total land. Twenty percent or more is an A, 10 to 19 percent is a B, 5 to 9 percent is a C, and under 5 percent is a D.

Minnesota clears the line. DNR says about 24 percent of the state is public land, more than 12 million acres of state, federal, and county ground. DNR itself manages 5.6 million acres, about 11 percent of the state. Counties manage another 2.8 million acres of tax-forfeited land, about 6 percent. That includes more than 1,500 wildlife management areas covering almost 1.4 million acres.

**The fine print, and it's a big one:** This A is a statewide grade, and much of the public ground is forest in the north. DNR's own materials say public land matters most in western and southern Minnesota because most of the land there is private. We didn't find a public-land share for the farm counties alone. If we had graded only the corn-and-bean country, we expect this grade would be lower.

There's one more way in, and it's aimed at farm country. Minnesota's Walk-In Access program paid landowners $18 per acre in 2025, the latest rate we could confirm, to open parcels of 40 acres or more with good wildlife cover, often CRP ground.

For 2026, about 22,000 acres on more than 220 sites are enrolled across the west, central, and southern parts of the state. The sites are open September 1 through May 31, and hunters need a $3 validation. The program started with pheasants in mind, so don't count on it being deer ground.

It matters to a leaseholder because it sets a comparison. A landowner weighing your offer can see what the state pays for open access, and your lease has to beat it or offer something it doesn't.

## Herd Health: B-minus

**How we graded it:** CWD and EHD results. An A is no wild CWD and no big EHD losses. A B means CWD is present but confined to a few counties and shows up in about 1 percent or less of tested deer, with a minus for a regional disease hit. A C means CWD is widespread or a region loses deer to EHD year after year. A D is both.

**CWD:** DNR's page for the 2025 season shows 14,639 samples and 124 positives, which is about 0.85 percent. The season before, it was 96 positives in 12,970 samples, about 0.74 percent. Positives have climbed from 63 in 2023 to 96 in 2024 to 124. Most are in the southeast. DPA 646 near Winona had the most in the early count, with 44.

Under 1 percent would earn a plain B on our scale. We took a minus for the direction.

In January 2025, DNR said CWD had reached an endemic stage in three southeast permit areas and ended targeted culling there. That same month it confirmed first positives in two new permit areas, one near Hawley and one near Greenfield. In 2025, a buck tested positive in McLeod County, outside any management zone. And after finds in Hennepin and Clay counties, the zone list reaches well beyond the southeast.

**EHD:** It's a small factor so far. DNR's page says EHD first hit Minnesota's wild deer in 2019, in Stearns and Houston counties. The first confirmed bluetongue cases in wild deer came in 2024. We didn't find a 2025 loss count.

**The fine print:** DNR tests heavily inside management zones, so the percentage is not a statewide infection rate. It tells you CWD is under 1 percent of the deer tested, not under 1 percent of the deer on your lease. For the basics, read [our CWD explainer](/blog/chronic-wasting-disease-explained/) and check DNR's CWD results map for the DPA you want.

## Lease Market and Law: C-plus

**How we graded it:** Two halves. One is Minnesota's landowner liability law, and whether it still protects a landowner who accepts a lease payment. The other is cropland cost against the national average, since land cost pushes lease prices up. Our grade averages the two halves. On cropland, within about 15 percent of the national average is an A, 15 to 30 percent over is a B, 30 to 50 percent over is a C, and more than 50 percent over is a D.

**The law drops away when you pay (law half: C).** Minnesota's recreational use law is Minnesota Statutes sections 604A.20 through 604A.27. It says a landowner who lets someone use the land for recreation, which includes hunting, without charge doesn't promise the land is safe, and doesn't take on liability for what the visitor does. Under the older text we read, the landowner owes no duty of care beyond not willfully causing injury.

But section 604A.25 says those protections don't limit the liability that would otherwise exist for injuries where the landowner charges people to come on the land for recreation. It carves out one kind of payment: money a landowner gets from the state or a local government for leasing or dedicating the land isn't a charge. A hunter's lease check isn't covered by that carve-out.

In plain terms, Minnesota's shield is for free access, which puts it with Indiana and Iowa. A landowner who charges for access may face a claim the law would otherwise block. That's our reading of the statute, not legal advice. We read an older published version, so check the current Minnesota Statutes before you sign anything.

**Cropland is costly, but not Iowa-costly (cropland half: B).** USDA's 2026 land values report puts Minnesota cropland at $7,300 per acre, up 4.3 percent from 2025. The U.S. average is $6,020, so Minnesota runs about 21 percent higher. Iowa is at $10,700, Illinois $10,200, Ohio $10,100, Indiana $8,600, and Michigan $6,600.

A C for the law and a B for cropland average to 2.5, which we round down to a C-plus, the same way we rounded Ohio.

**The fine print:** We couldn't find an official Minnesota per-acre hunting lease price. One leasing marketplace, HLRBO, says its Minnesota listings average $20 to $25 per acre, with strong ground at $40 or more. That's a marketplace's own number, not a survey. For the ranges we've found across the Corn Belt, see [what hunters actually pay to lease ground](/blog/cost-of-leasing-ag-ground/). Get anything you sign reviewed by a lawyer.

## What the Grades Mean If You Lease in Minnesota

- **Look up the DPA first.** The antlerless limit and any CWD rules follow the permit area, not the farm gate.
- **Expect a signed release.** Because the law doesn't shield a landowner who charges, expect to be asked for a written release and proof of liability insurance. See [what to look for when leasing hunting land](/blog/leasing-hunting-property-what-to-look-for/).
- **Don't plan on public ground in farm country.** The 24 percent is real, but much of it is up north. In the south and west, plan on private ground and a handful of WMAs.
- **Ask the landowner about Walk-In Access.** The state paid $18 per acre in 2025. Your offer has to beat that or add something the program doesn't.
- **Check the county before you buy a rifle.** Thirteen counties kept shotgun-only rules for 2026, and the list can change.
- **Check the CWD map for the farm's DPA.** Mandatory testing moves to a new part of the state each year, and positives keep turning up in new places.
- **Check the dates and the license costs.** Our [Minnesota deer season dates page](/minnesota-deer-season-dates/) has this year's seasons, and our [7-state license guide](/blog/deer-hunting-license-guide-seven-state/) has the costs.

## FAQ: Minnesota Deer Hunting Leases

### Is Minnesota a good state to lease deer ground?

For access, yes. About 24 percent of the state is public land, and the harvest is back above average. Bucks are good but not elite, at seventh in Boone and Crockett's 2022 to 2024 entries. CWD is spreading, and the state's liability law doesn't protect a landowner who charges a lease fee.

### How much does it cost to lease hunting land in Minnesota?

We couldn't find an official Minnesota per-acre lease price. One leasing marketplace, HLRBO, reports Minnesota listings averaging $20 to $25 per acre, with stronger ground at $40 or more. The state's Walk-In Access program paid landowners $18 per acre in 2025 to open land to the public, so that's the number a landowner may compare your offer to.

### Is a landowner protected from liability if they lease land to hunters in Minnesota?

Not under the recreational use law. Minnesota Statutes 604A.20 to 604A.27 protect a landowner who allows recreation without charge, and section 604A.25 says that doesn't limit liability for injuries when the landowner charges for access. A written lease, a signed release, and insurance are the usual safeguards. Talk to a lawyer before signing.

### How many deer can I take on a Minnesota lease?

Minnesota allows one buck per license year, statewide. Antlerless deer are set by Deer Permit Area, with limits that run from bucks-only and lottery up to five deer in some areas. Check the DPA for the farm before you lease.

## The Bottom Line

Minnesota gets a B (3.0). That's ahead of Michigan's B-minus (2.9), Ohio's B-minus (2.8), Indiana's C-plus (2.5), and Iowa's C (2.0). It's the best grade in the series so far, but read it carefully. The Access A is a statewide number, and much of that public ground is in the north woods. Grade only the farm country in the south and west and Minnesota would drop, so don't read the order as a ranking.

If you lease in Minnesota, look up the permit area first and the scouting second. Check the antlerless limit and the CWD rules, get the release and the insurance in writing, and don't count on public ground near the farm.
