You already found [the right fence line to hunt](/blog/reading-a-fence-line). Now comes the part that actually matters: your **fence-line stand setup**. A stand on an open ag edge isn't like a stand in the timber. Set it up the same way, and you'll blow out a good fence line in week one.

## Go Higher Than You Would in Timber

In the timber, height mostly hides your shape from deer looking through trees and brush. On a fence line next to open crop, there's no cover behind you. You're just a shape against the sky. That means you need more height, not less.

- **20 feet, minimum**, on fence posts, tall trees, or anywhere you've got real cover behind you — a tree line, a grain bin, a dip in the land
- **Go to 22–25 feet** if the crop next to you is already cut. Bare stubble gives deer a clear look at you, and nothing to break up your outline
- **A tree that's still holding its leaves beats a taller bare tree.** Once you're above 18 feet or so, having cover around you matters more than raw height

## Your Walk In and Out Decides Everything

On small leased ground, you probably don't get to pick from three or four stand spots. You get one fence line and one way to reach it. [What you're actually agreeing to when you lease that ground](/blog/leasing-hunting-property-what-to-look-for) shapes how much flexibility you have here, too — smaller, tighter leases mean fewer backup options if a stand gets burned.

- **Walk in through the crop, not through the cover,** when the field rotation lets you. A cut or standing field is quieter to move through than brush. It also keeps your scent away from the side of the fence where deer bed
- **Never cross the fence line to reach your stand.** That's the exact trail you're trying not to spook
- **If your only way in crosses open ground, walk it in the dark.** Do this on morning sits too. On a small property, you don't have room to make a mistake and just move to a different stand next time

## Wind Runs Straighter Out in the Open

A fence line with crop on both sides doesn't have timber to block or bend the wind. It runs straighter and more predictably than in the woods. That's good once you know how to use it, and bad if you're still reading wind like you're in the timber. If you haven't already, [building a preseason wind chart for your stands](/blog/building-a-preseason-wind-chart) makes this call a lot easier before season pressure makes guessing costly.

- **Set up so the wind blows across the fence line, not down it.** A stand with wind running straight down the fence carries your scent to every deer using that fence for a quarter mile
- **Morning wind still pulls downhill, even on flat-looking ground.** Small dips and rises matter more out in the open, since nothing else slows the air down before it reaches a bedded deer
- **Check the wind at your stand, not back at the house.** A mile of open corn can shift the wind more than you'd think between your truck and your tree

## One Stand, So Don't Waste It

Small leased ground usually means you don't have a backup stand to fall back on if this one gets burned. That's why the walk in, the walk out, and the wind check aren't optional here — they're the whole game. Blow one approach on a 40-acre lease with a single good fence line, and you could lose the rest of your season on that ground.

## FAQ: Fence-Line Stand Setups

### How high should a treestand be on an open fence line?

At least 20 feet if you've got real cover behind you, like a tree line or a grain bin. Go to 22–25 feet if the crop next to your stand is already cut, since bare stubble gives deer a clear look at you with nothing to break up your outline.

### Should I walk through the crop or the cover to reach my stand?

Walk through the crop when the field rotation allows it. A cut or standing field is quieter to move through than brush, and it keeps your scent away from the side of the fence where deer are bedding.

### Why does wind behave differently on a fence line than in the timber?

There's no timber to block or bend it. Wind runs straighter and more predictably down an open edge, which means a stand with wind blowing straight down the fence line carries your scent much farther than it would in the woods.

### What if I only have one good stand location on my leased ground?

Treat every approach like it's your only shot, because it might be. Small leased parcels often don't have a backup stand to fall back on, so a blown entry or exit route can cost you the rest of the season on that ground.

## The Bottom Line

Picking the right fence line gets you halfway there. The stand itself — height, entry, exit, and wind — is what decides whether that edge actually produces or gets blown out in the first week. On small leased ground especially, you usually don't get a second shot at it.

If that fence line borders corn ground with a split silage-and-grain harvest nearby, plan on setting up here twice in one fall, not once — see [Ohio's Two-Stage Corn Harvest](/blog/ohio-two-stage-corn-harvest-deer-hunting) for how to time the second reset.
