Michigan has plenty of public hunting land, and almost none of it sits where the row crops are. That split is the story of the **Michigan deer hunting lease** market, and the report card below puts a grade on each piece of it.

This is the second card in our Ag-Lease Whitetail Report Card series. Every state gets graded on the same five categories with the same ruler, so the grades can be compared. [Ohio went first](/blog/ohio-ag-lease-whitetail-report-card/). Michigan is up now.

Every grade rests on a published number, and we show the number. The cutoffs for what counts as an A or a C are ours, and we list them. A few of Michigan's numbers were harder to pin down than Ohio's, and where that happened, we say so.

## Michigan Deer Hunting Lease Report Card: The Grades

**Overall: B-minus.** Lots of public ground and cheap farmland, held back by thin record-book bucks, a spreading CWD map, and a landowner liability rule that's tougher on paid leases.

## Buck Quality: C

**How we graded it:** Boone and Crockett's ranking of states by whitetail entries. Top three is an A, fourth through seventh is a B, eighth through twelfth is a C, and anything lower is a D.

Michigan doesn't make Boone and Crockett's top ten for the latest window, January 1, 2022 to December 31, 2024. Texas is tenth with 60 entries. Ohio is third with 125, and Wisconsin leads with 135.

Boone and Crockett only publishes the top ten for that window, so we can't give you Michigan's exact count or rank. The last window where Michigan shows up is 2020 to 2022. It had 15 entries, which put it 13th on a list that includes Canadian provinces. Among states alone, that's 11th. Ohio had 107 in the same window, about seven times as many.

We put Michigan in the C band because 11th is where it last showed up. If its 2022 to 2024 count puts it lower than 12th among states, this grade drops to a D.

**The fine print:** Record-book entries count the very top of the herd, not the average buck on your lease. Michigan hunters take plenty of bucks, with 153,670 antlered deer reported in 2025. Whether those bucks are older is a different question. If you want to know why ag-country bucks can look older than they are, see [our Field Note on that](/blog/why-ag-country-bucks-look-older-than-they-are/).

## Deer Numbers: B

**How we graded it:** The latest season's harvest against the three-year average, and where it sits in the record books. Above average and in the top ten seasons ever is an A. Within 10 percent below average is a B. Ten to 25 percent below is a C. Anything worse is a D.

This is the category where Michigan's numbers are messy, so here's the full picture.

Michigan hunters reported 295,739 deer in 2025, according to the DNR's harvest summary as of February 1, 2026. That's 153,670 antlered and 142,069 antlerless. It's a hair under 2024's early reported count of 299,492.

The DNR says reported counts run low. Its follow-up survey raised the 2024 total by nearly 75,000, to an estimate of 373,300 deer. That was up 7.6 percent from the survey estimate of 347,008 in 2023.

We couldn't find a DNR survey estimate for 2025, so we can't compare it to the survey numbers yet. To get a fair comparison, we used reported counts only. The DNR's reported harvest was about 303,000 in 2022, the first year of mandatory online reporting, and about 274,000 in 2023. Add 2024's 299,492 and the three-year average is about 292,000. The 2025 count is about 1 percent above that, by our math.

That's above average. We couldn't find a published ranking of Michigan's biggest harvest years, so we couldn't confirm a top-ten season, and that's why this is a B and not an A.

**The fine print:** This grade is provisional. When the DNR publishes its 2025 survey estimate, we'll check it and update the card. The DNR also says reported counts always run lower than the real number, so treat every figure here as a floor.

## Access: A

**How we graded it:** Public land as a share of the state's total land. Twenty percent or more is an A, 10 to 19 percent is a B, 5 to 9 percent is a C, and under 5 percent is a D.

In 2017, Michigan's DNR said about 21 percent of the state is public land. That clears the A line by a point. The DNR itself manages roughly 4.6 million acres, including 3.9 million acres of state forest and 364,000 acres of state game and wildlife areas.

**The fine print:** Here's the catch. The same DNR source says public land is about 3 percent of the southern Lower Peninsula. That's where most of the row crops are, and where most of the people live. The big public tracts are up north and in the Upper Peninsula. Michigan's A is real, but it's for the state as a whole. If your ag-land hunting is in the south, you're in a lease market that looks a lot like Ohio's.

There's one more way in. The DNR's Hunting Access Program (HAP) pays private landowners to open their ground to public hunters, for free, on a first-come basis. It's open to landowners with 20 or more acres in the lower peninsula and part of the eastern Upper Peninsula. It pays up to $30 per acre for high-quality habitat, such as CRP ground or a food plot. Leases run one year, and payments come at the end of the season. Availability varies by county, and we couldn't find how many acres are enrolled today.

HAP matters for a leaseholder because it's a price tag. A landowner who's weighing your offer can compare it to what the state would pay.

## Herd Health: C-plus

**How we graded it:** CWD and EHD results. An A is no wild CWD and no big EHD losses. A B means CWD is present but confined to a few counties and shows up in about 1 percent or less of tested deer, with a minus for a regional disease hit. A C means CWD is widespread or a region loses deer to EHD year after year. A D is both.

**CWD:** Allegan County became the 17th Michigan county with CWD in wild deer on November 13, 2025. The others are Clinton, Dickinson, Eaton, Genesee, Gratiot, Hillsdale, Ingham, Ionia, Isabella, Jackson, Kent, Mecosta, Midland, Montcalm, Ogemaw, and Washtenaw. Three of them (Washtenaw, Genesee, and Allegan) reported their first positives in 2025, and they're in the southern half of the Lower Peninsula, in the farm country this series is about.

Since 2002, DNR surveillance has found 265 CWD-positive deer. Hunter-submitted samples have added 61 more since 2020, by the DNR's count in that same release. That's about 326 combined, by our math. Of the 1,467 deer tested in 2025 with final results, 6 were positive, or about 0.4 percent.

**EHD:** The DNR confirmed epizootic hemorrhagic disease in Eaton, Jackson, Van Buren, and Washtenaw counties in September 2025. The DNR says EHD outbreaks haven't been shown to have a significant long-term effect on Michigan's deer population. It also says losses can be intense in small areas, and local effects can last a few years.

**Why C-plus and not B-minus:** The testing rate is low, and EHD is local. But 17 of Michigan's 83 counties isn't "a few," and the recent spread is into the south. That's what holds this grade down.

**The fine print:** The 0.4 percent number is the share of deer that were tested, not the share of the herd. Read [our CWD explainer](/blog/chronic-wasting-disease-explained/) and check the DNR's current CWD page before you lease in or near the counties above.

## Lease Market and Law: B

**How we graded it:** Two halves. One is Michigan's landowner liability law, and what happens to a landowner's protection once a hunter pays. The other is cropland cost against the national average, since land cost pushes lease prices up. A law that holds up under a lease payment, plus cropland within about 15 percent of the national average, is an A on that half. A narrower law is a B. Our grade averages the two halves. Ohio's card used the same two halves: a strong law and cropland more than 50 percent over the national average worked out to a C-plus.

**The law has a catch for paid leases.** Michigan's recreational land use statute, MCL 324.73301, protects landowners from injury claims by people on their land. For people who *don't* pay to be there, the owner is only liable for gross negligence or willful and wanton misconduct.

A hunter who pays is different. For a paying hunter on a farm, the statute says no claim arises unless the injury came from a condition that was an unreasonable risk of harm, and all three of these are true: the owner knew or had reason to know about it, the owner failed to use reasonable care to fix it or warn about it, and the hunter didn't know and had no reason to know about it.

In plain terms, a landowner who takes a lease check is still protected, but by a narrower test. A hidden hazard the landowner knew about and didn't mention can be a problem. Ohio's lease law gives landowners a stronger shield, so Michigan comes in lower here. That's our reading of the statute, not legal advice.

**The land is cheap, compared to Ohio.** USDA's 2026 land values report puts Michigan cropland at $6,600 per acre, up 3.9 percent from 2025. The U.S. average is $6,020. Michigan is about 10 percent higher, by our math. Ohio's cropland is $10,100. When ground costs less, a landowner has less reason to price out a hunter.

**The fine print:** We couldn't find any published Michigan hunting lease price per acre, so this grade doesn't use one. For the ranges we did find across the Corn Belt, see [what hunters actually pay to lease ground](/blog/cost-of-leasing-ag-ground/). Michigan Farm Bureau tells landowners the act's protections "are not absolute." Get anything you sign reviewed by a lawyer.

## What the Grades Mean If You Lease in Michigan

- **Pick your half of the state first.** The A for access is mostly an up-north and Upper Peninsula story. In the southern Lower Peninsula, you're leasing, and it's a lease market.
- **Check the CWD map before you sign.** The newest counties are in the south, so a lease that was clear last year might not be now.
- **Ask the landowner about HAP.** The state's payment is a number they already know. Your offer, and what you'll do on the ground, has to beat it or add something it doesn't.
- **Put the liability talk in writing.** Michigan's law treats a paid hunter differently, so a lease should spell out who hunts, where, and how hazards get reported. For what to check on a parcel, see [what to look for when leasing hunting land](/blog/leasing-hunting-property-what-to-look-for/).
- **Check the dates before you plan.** Our [Michigan deer season dates page](/michigan-deer-season-dates/) has this year's seasons and rifle rules.

## FAQ: Michigan Deer Hunting Leases

### Is Michigan a good state to lease deer ground?

It depends on which half. Michigan has about 21 percent public land statewide, and cropland costs only about 10 percent above the national average. But public land is only about 3 percent of the southern Lower Peninsula, where most of the row crops are. Michigan didn't make Boone and Crockett's top ten whitetail states for 2022 to 2024, so the very big bucks are rarer than in Ohio, Indiana, or Wisconsin.

### How much does it cost to lease hunting land in Michigan?

We couldn't find a published Michigan-specific price per acre. Our Corn Belt lease cost piece found most decent ag-adjacent leases between $20 and $40 per acre per year, with a wide range around that. Michigan cropland is valued at $6,600 per acre, close to the U.S. average, so lease prices may run lower than in higher-priced states. The state's Hunting Access Program pays landowners up to $30 per acre for the best habitat.

### Is a landowner protected from liability if they lease land to hunters in Michigan?

Mostly, but by a narrower test than for free entry. Under MCL 324.73301, a landowner can still be liable to a paying hunter if the owner knew of an unreasonable risk, didn't fix it or warn about it, and the hunter didn't know about it. Landowners who enroll in the state's HAP program get a gross-negligence standard. Talk to a lawyer before signing a lease.

### Does CWD affect hunting leases in Michigan?

It can. CWD has been found in wild deer in 17 Michigan counties, and three of the newest ones (Washtenaw, Genesee, and Allegan) are in the southern Lower Peninsula. Rules and testing requests vary by area. Check the DNR's current CWD page before you lease.

## The Bottom Line

Michigan gets a B-minus, a tenth of a point ahead of Ohio. That gap is small enough that one different call on a grade would flip it, so don't read it as a ranking. The two states fail in opposite ways. Ohio has the deer and no ground. Michigan has the ground, but not always where the row crops are.
