Here's something we keep coming back to on this site: a bigger cartridge doesn't fix a bad hit. Neither does a better scope, a lighter rifle, or a fancier bullet. Straight-wall rifle shot placement comes from time behind the rifle, and that part doesn't come in the box.

Gear gets you to the starting line. Form and fundamentals get you the deer. That means a steady rest, easy breathing, a smooth trigger squeeze, and the discipline to pass when the shot isn't right. We said it in our [.400 Legend vs. .350 Legend](/blog/350-legend-vs-400-legend/) comparison, where raw energy means nothing if you flinch, and in our [late-August sighting-in checklist](/blog/sighting-in-straight-wall-rifle-late-august-checklist/), which says to shoot from the position you'll actually hunt from, not just off a bench.

So before we talk about where to aim, here's the part that comes first: go to the range. Then go again. Practice, practice, practice. A modest rifle you shoot well beats an expensive one you barely touch. If recoil is what keeps you from shooting more, [Low-Recoil Straight-Wall Rifles for Youth Hunters](/blog/low-recoil-rifles-youth-beginners/) covers the easiest-shooting options.

A straight-wall rifle is built for the way most of us hunt ag ground: a field edge, a shot inside 150 yards, and a deer that never knew you were there. That's a good setup, and it's one where your practice pays off. Here's the plain version. Where to aim, what the one caliber-specific warning we found actually says, and how long to wait before you go looking.

## Where to Aim a Straight-Wall Rifle on a Deer

The target is the lungs. Hunter-Ed describes the double-lung zone as about the size of a volleyball, and calls it the largest vital target, with the widest margin for error. It sits just behind the front shoulder.

To find it, locate the front leg and picture a line running up from just behind it. onX's guide says to aim in line with the front leg, somewhere between the halfway mark and the lower third of the body. Hunter-Ed puts it about a third of the way up from the bottom of the chest. Same spot, two ways of saying it.

How you aim depends on the angle:

- **Broadside:** Your best shot. Aim behind the shoulder, in that zone.
- **Quartering away:** Still good. Hunter-Ed says to aim for the far shoulder, and onX says that's where you want the bullet to exit.
- **Quartering toward:** Hunter-Ed says experienced hunters only. onX goes further for rifles, warning that you're "most certainly going to damage the front shoulder meat plus do substantial damage to the guts."
- **Facing you or straight away:** Skip it. Both guides steer you away from head-on and tailhead shots.
- **Neck, head, spine:** Hunter-Ed says these often result in lost animals.

If you pass on a bad angle, you lose a few minutes. If you take it, you might lose the deer.

## The Straight-Wall Part: Bullet Weight and Distance

Here's where a straight-wall rifle is different. These cartridges move slower than a bottleneck rifle, and a bullet slows down more the farther it travels. A bullet needs speed to open up, kind of like a water balloon needs a hard throw to burst. Toss it gently and it just bounces.

We didn't find controlled studies comparing straight-wall bullets at distance, so we won't pretend to have numbers. What we did find is a field report. David Hoffman of Iowa Blood Trackers, writing in The Iowa Sportsman in 2023, named the .350 Legend as a wounding concern, especially with 125-grain bullets in youth season. He wrote that lighter bullets "are typically producing poor blood trails especially at longer ranges over 100+ yards." Heavier bullets of 180 grains or more, he said, "appear to perform better."

That's one tracker's observation, not a lab test, so treat it that way. It lines up with a complaint we covered in [The .400 Legend Is Catching On: 2023 to Now](/blog/400-legend-rise/), where inconsistent blood trails came up past 100 to 150 yards. A bigger case doesn't fix a bad hit, though. That part still comes down to where you aim.

What to do with it:

- **Pick a heavier bullet if you can.** For the .350 Legend, that means 180 grains or more, per Hoffman's observation.
- **Know your velocity at your distance.** The [Ballistics Comparison Tool](/ballistics-comparison/) shows velocity and energy at set yardages for up to four loads. Check what your load is doing at the distance you actually shoot.
- **Ask your ammo maker.** Find out how fast the bullet needs to be going to expand, then compare that to what the tool shows.
- **Shoot inside your range, not your limit.** Our full breakdown is in [Straight-Wall vs Shouldered Ballistics: Why It's Not Simple](/blog/straight-wall-vs-shouldered-ballistics-comparison/).

## After the Shot: Mark It, Then Wait

Stay put and replay it. Westerfield, writing for the National Deer Association, says it's important to pinpoint where the deer was standing when you shot and the last place you saw it. Listen for the deer going down, too.

On ag ground, here's a simple habit. Pick a landmark you can find again, like a fence post, a row count from the end of the field, or a single odd tree. Standing corn and tall stubble all look alike once you're walking in them.

Then wait. The numbers below come from two sources that don't fully agree, which is worth knowing.

- **Lung or heart with a rifle:** Westerfield waits at least 30 minutes, and says the deer should be found dead within about 150 yards.
- **Liver:** Westerfield waits at least 2 hours, sometimes 3 or 4. Hoffman's guideline is 6 to 9 hours.
- **Gut:** Westerfield waits 4 to 6 hours if rain isn't coming. Hoffman says times vary widely, with some deer dead inside 4 hours and others still alive at 32.

Hoffman calls tracking too soon the main reason mortally wounded deer travel a long distance. His rule is "When in doubt, back out." If the sign points to liver or gut, our read is to lean toward the longer numbers.

## Reading the Blood

Westerfield's quick guide:

- **Crimson and frothy:** Lungs, and usually a decent trail.
- **Bright red:** Possibly heart, possibly a leg wound.
- **Dark red:** Usually liver.
- **Green or brown material with an odor:** Gut.

He also sprays suspected drops with hydrogen peroxide. Real blood fizzes. Mark each drop with toilet paper so you can see which way the trail runs.

## When the Blood Runs Out

Here's the second place the experts split. Westerfield suggests a grid search when blood runs out, with friends spaced so everyone stays in sight of each other. Hoffman says don't grid search, because it can lay false scent trails, and wait for a tracking dog if one is coming. Dogs are legal in some states and not others, so check your state's rule before you count on one.

Either way, if you're leasing, call the landowner before you cross a fence or head onto a neighboring property. Don't just start walking.

## FAQ: Shot Placement and Tracking

### Can a bigger cartridge make up for poor shooting?

No. A bigger cartridge doesn't fix a bad hit. Range time, a steady rest, and good fundamentals matter more than the gear, and the best practice is from the position you'll actually hunt from.

### Where should you aim on a deer with a straight-wall rifle?

Behind the front shoulder, about a third to halfway up the body, in line with the front leg. That's the lungs, the biggest vital target.

### How long should you wait before tracking a deer shot with a rifle?

For a lung or heart hit, the National Deer Association's Bob Westerfield waits at least 30 minutes. For a liver or gut hit, wait hours, not minutes. Sources range from 2 to 9 hours for liver.

### Does the .350 Legend leave poor blood trails?

An Iowa tracker reported poor blood trails with lighter 125-grain bullets past 100 yards, and said 180-grain or heavier bullets appear to perform better. That's a field observation, not a controlled study.

### Should you grid search for a lost deer?

Experts disagree. Westerfield recommends a grid search with helpers. Hoffman says it can create false scent trails and recommends waiting for a tracking dog. Check your state's rules on dogs.

### What if a wounded deer crosses onto a neighbor's land?

Contact the landowner for permission before you go onto their property.

## The Bottom Line

A straight-wall rifle does its best work on a broadside deer inside the range your bullet still opens up. Aim for the lungs, pick a heavier bullet, know your velocity at your distance, then mark the spot and wait. Patience is what recovers deer.
