**A food plot beats a mineral site for one simple reason: it's legal everywhere, all season long. A mineral site often isn't.** A planted food plot counts as farming in every state we cover. A pile of corn, salt, or mineral counts as bait — and bait rules are a mess. Some states ban it outright. Some allow it only in certain counties. One state might change its rule this year. If you're putting time or lease money into pulling deer onto your ground, put it into the thing that won't get pulled out from under you.

This isn't about deer needing more food. On leased row-crop ground, deer already have plenty to eat — see [Food Plots Are Optional, Sanctuary Acres Aren't](/blog/sanctuary-acres) for why a safe place to bed down usually matters more than a food source. This article is about something different: legal risk. When food really does help — like holding deer on your ground after the corn and beans come off — a food plot gets you there without the risk a mineral site carries.

## The simple rule behind every state's law

Every state on this list draws the same basic line, even if the wording is different:

- **Stuff you *place* — corn piles, mineral blocks, salt, deer attractants — counts as bait.**
- **Stuff you *grow* — a planted food plot, a standing crop — counts as farming, not bait.**

That's the whole idea. What changes state to state is how strict the bait rule is, and what happens if you break it.

| State | Mineral or bait piles | Food plots |
|---|---|---|
| **Ohio** | Legal on private land — *except* inside a Disease Surveillance Area (a zone set up around a sick-deer finding) | Always legal |
| **Michigan — Lower Peninsula** | Not legal, at all, since 2019 | Always legal |
| **Michigan — Upper Peninsula** | Legal, with limits (set dates, 2-gallon cap, must be far from houses) | Always legal |
| **Indiana** | Not legal to hunt over | Always legal — IDNR says it's fine to hunt near one |
| **Iowa** | Not legal to hunt over | Always legal |
| **Illinois** | Not legal at any time | Always legal |
| **Minnesota** | Not legal to hunt over, statewide. Some counties also ban plain feeding, even outside hunting season | Always legal |
| **Wisconsin** | Legal only in counties without a CWD ban — and that list changes often | Always legal, in every county |

A few things worth knowing:

- **Ohio is the one state on this list where a mineral site is usually fine.** A lot of websites get this wrong and say Ohio bans it everywhere. That's not true, except inside a Disease Surveillance Area. If your lease is anywhere near one of those zones, check with ODNR before you assume the normal rule applies to you.
- **Michigan could change soon.** A bill to allow baiting again in the Lower Peninsula passed the state House back in February 2026. It's still sitting in the Senate and hasn't become law. The DNR is against it. Until it passes — if it ever does — the ban stays in place. Don't plan your season around a bill that hasn't passed yet.
- **Wisconsin is the trickiest one to track.** The rule depends on your county, and it changes on its own: any county with a confirmed sick deer gets a 2- or 3-year bait ban, and it resets if another sick deer turns up. Check your specific county before season, every year.
- **Indiana, Iowa, and Illinois are the simplest.** Bait is out, food plots are in, no exceptions worth worrying about.
- **Minnesota has two separate rules.** One says you can't hunt over bait, anywhere in the state. A second, wider rule bans plain feeding (not tied to hunting at all) in dozens of counties, and that list grows most years as more CWD cases turn up.

*A note on accuracy: this table is built from official state wildlife agency sources, checked in August 2026. Bait rules move fast, especially anything tied to sick-deer cases — sometimes the agency changes them mid-season without waiting on the legislature. Double-check your state's site before you put anything out this fall.*

## Why a food plot still wins, even where mineral sites are legal

Ohio and the Upper Peninsula of Michigan are the two places on this list where a mineral site is a real option. Even there, a food plot is usually still the better move. Here's why:

- **A food plot survives a lease change. A mineral site doesn't.** A mineral site is something you build and maintain yourself. A food plot can go right into next year's lease agreement, and the plant matter left behind still has value even if nobody replants it.
- **A food plot works when it matters most — gun season.** Most of a mineral site's value happens in early summer, while antlers are growing and you're checking trail cameras. By the time firearms season opens, that value has usually faded. A food plot full of brassicas or grain is still drawing deer in December.
- **One new sick-deer case can wipe out a mineral site overnight.** Disease zones only get added, almost never removed, and they can show up with little warning. A food plot isn't touched by any of that.

## What this means for your leased ground

If you're working a 40- to 160-acre leased parcel and deciding where to spend your food-plot-or-mineral-site effort this year, the legal picture points one way: a food plot survives a lease change, doesn't depend on a disease map or a state Senate vote, and does its best work exactly when you need it — after harvest, through the tail end of the season. If you haven't locked in next year's terms yet, see [What to Look for When Leasing Hunting Land](/blog/leasing-hunting-property-what-to-look-for) for what else is worth putting in writing.

That doesn't change the bigger point from [Food Plots Are Optional, Sanctuary Acres Aren't](/blog/sanctuary-acres): on most leased ground, a no-entry sanctuary acre still moves the needle more than any food source will. But if you're already planning a plot, or trying to decide between a plot and a mineral site, the legal case settles it in the plot's favor almost everywhere — and even in Ohio, it's close.
