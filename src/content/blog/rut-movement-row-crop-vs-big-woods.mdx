Picture two hunters in the same week of November. One is on 120 acres of leased beans and corn with a 15-acre woodlot. The other is deep in unbroken hardwoods, miles from the nearest field. Both have heard "the rut is on." But rut movement doesn't look the same in those two places, and hunting both the same way is how you end up sitting in the wrong spot.

This Field Note covers what GPS studies say about how bucks move during the rut, and what changes between farm country and big woods. One honest note first: we didn't find a study that measured the same thing in both landscapes. So we'll show you what each study found, and mark where we're connecting the dots ourselves.

## Rut Movement Timing: What Stays the Same

The timing of the rut doesn't change with the landscape. Day length drives it, not what's growing around the deer.

A 2025 study in southwest Wisconsin tracked 188 GPS-collared bucks from 2017 to 2020. The area is mostly deciduous forest mixed with farmland, in parts of Dane, Iowa, and Grant counties. The researchers put the peak of breeding between Oct. 23 and Nov. 12, and the busiest week of buck activity was Nov. 5 to 11.

Our own [rut timing guide](/blog/when-does-the-rut-start/) has the state numbers. Ohio's peak breeding window is Nov. 11 to 17. Iowa's is the first half of November. Use your state's dates, not Wisconsin's.

## What Changes: How Far a Buck Has to Go

Here's where the two landscapes pull apart. The best evidence we found is a Pennsylvania study that GPS-collared 61 adult deer from 2009 to 2015 across areas ranging from farm country to solid forest.

Home ranges got bigger as the forest got more continuous. In the fragmented, farm-mixed Gettysburg-Newark Lowland, the average range was 1.26 square kilometers, or about 310 acres. In the most forested area, Deep Valleys, it was 2.79 square kilometers, about 690 acres. That's more than twice as big. Males had much bigger ranges than females.

**The fine print:** Those are year-round ranges for both sexes, not rut ranges for bucks. The study also had a limited number of males. And it's Pennsylvania, not the Corn Belt. It shows the pattern, not your county's numbers.

So a deer in a patchwork of fields and small woodlots has a smaller area to cover than one in a solid block of timber. That's the baseline.

## What a Rut Trip Looks Like on Farm Ground

Even in broken-up country, bucks leave home during the rut. A Maryland study at Chesapeake Farms, run by researchers from North Carolina State University, GPS-collared 32 adult bucks from 2003 to 2007. Sixty-three percent of them made at least one trip outside their home range right before or during the breeding season.

Those trips weren't epics. The average trip reached at least about 780 meters out, which is roughly half a mile, and lasted about 10 and a half hours. The researchers said breeding was the driving force behind most of them. They judged hunting pressure and food unlikely causes.

The study paper describes the farm as about half forest, 20 percent cash crops, 13 percent fallow fields and food plots, and 17 percent other wildlife cover and waterfowl impoundments. That's close to a lot of Midwest ground. Dr. Dave Samuel's summary of a related NC State study of 15 adult bucks adds that their November core areas were often where does bedded.

For comparison, the Noble Foundation's study in southern Oklahoma found rutting bucks averaged 4.6 miles a day, moving in straighter lines that point to a buck seeking does. That's a different region and a different landscape, so it isn't a fair farm-versus-woods comparison. What it shows is that distance depends on the landscape, and the Noble researchers say so too.

## When Bucks Move: Age Matters

Back to Wisconsin, because it's the closest to our ground. The study found two-year-old bucks moved the most per hour. Bucks 3 and older showed the most variation from buck to buck.

The older bucks also shifted their schedule. At dawn, bucks 3 and older moved less than the younger ones. At night, two-year-olds and bucks 3 and older moved more than yearlings. The researchers wrote that older bucks were "transitioning towards nocturnal activity patterns during the peak rut." In daylight, yearlings and two-year-olds moved slightly more than the older bucks.

That's a hard fact for hunters. Your oldest bucks may be shifting toward night just when you most want them up and moving. The difference in daylight was slight, so don't write off the daytime, but don't count on it either. Opening weekend of gun season, by the way, had no significant effect on movement rates in that study.

- **Think cover block to cover block.** If rut trips are about a half mile, a buck on farm ground is hopping between woodlots, CRP, waterways, and creek bottoms. The lines that connect them matter. Our guides on [terrain funnels](/blog/terrain-funnels-row-crop-country/) and [drainage ditches as travel corridors](/blog/drainage-ditches-as-travel-corridors/) cover how to find them.
- **Hunt where does are.** If bucks are searching, and their November core areas sit where does bed, doe bedding and the feed near it are the places to scout.
- **Favor cover edges over open field for the older buck.** If your best buck is shifting toward night, the cover edge in the last light is a better bet than the middle of a bean field. See [how to read a fence line](/blog/reading-a-fence-line/) for crossing points, and [our scrape guide](/blog/hunting-scrapes-field-edges-fence-lines/) for the pre-rut sign that goes with it.
- **Know what the combine did.** Crop cover changes where deer can go. See [Three Days After the Combine](/blog/three-days-after-combine/).

## What This Means in Big Woods

This is our reading too.

- **Expect bucks to be passing through.** With bigger ranges, a buck is more likely to cross your stand than to live near it. That puts a premium on features that force movement: saddles, creek crossings, the edge of a thick regrowth patch or clearcut.
- **Scout for sign you can actually repeat.** Scrapes and rubs show up on trails and in the interior of the woods too, not just on field edges. A line of sign is worth more than one scrape.
- **Plan for fewer sure things.** On farm ground, you can watch a field. In big woods, you can't, so the stand has to earn its spot with sign.

## What These Studies Can't Tell You

- **None of them measured both landscapes side by side.** We're comparing separate studies.
- **Wisconsin's cover is mixed.** The paper gives no breakdown of forest versus farmland.
- **Pennsylvania's ranges are annual, for both sexes.** They're not rut ranges for bucks.
- **Maryland's trips are about one farm.** It's a 32-buck sample from one property.
- **Oklahoma isn't the Midwest.** We used it only to show that landscape changes distance.

## FAQ: Rut Movement

### How far do bucks travel during the rut?

It depends on the landscape. In a Maryland farm-and-forest study, 63 percent of adult bucks took at least one trip outside their home range around the rut, going about half a mile for about 10 hours. In southern Oklahoma, bucks averaged 4.6 miles a day during the rut.

### Do bucks move more in daylight during the rut?

Younger bucks do slightly more than older ones. In a Wisconsin study of 188 bucks, yearlings and two-year-olds moved slightly more in daylight, while bucks 3 and older moved more toward night during peak rut.

### Is rut movement different on farm ground than in big woods?

Home ranges are. A Pennsylvania study found ranges more than twice as big in the most forested area as in the most farm-mixed one. We didn't find a study that compared rut movement in both landscapes directly.

### When does buck movement peak in the Midwest?

In a southwest Wisconsin study, the busiest week of buck movement was Nov. 5 to 11, and peak breeding ran Oct. 23 to Nov. 12. Ohio's peak breeding window is Nov. 11 to 17, and Iowa's is the first half of November.

## The Bottom Line

On farm ground, the rut is a short walk between small patches of cover. In big woods, it's a longer walk with fewer clues. Hunt the lines between cover on one, and the features that force movement on the other, and don't count on your oldest buck showing in daylight on either.
