Indiana grows some of the best bucks in the country, and almost none of the ground is public. That's the story of the **Indiana deer hunting lease** market, and it has a twist the other states don't: the state's liability law stops protecting the landowner the moment you pay. The report card below puts a grade on each piece.

This is the third card in our Ag-Lease Whitetail Report Card series. Every state gets graded on the same five categories with the same ruler, so the grades can be compared. [Ohio went first](/blog/ohio-ag-lease-whitetail-report-card/), then [Michigan](/blog/michigan-ag-lease-whitetail-report-card/). Indiana is up now.

Every grade rests on a published number, and we show the number. The cutoffs for what counts as an A or a C are ours, and we list them. A few of Indiana's numbers were harder to pin down than Ohio's, and where that happened, we say so.

## Indiana Deer Hunting Lease Report Card: The Grades

**Overall: C-plus.** Elite bucks and a healthy harvest, held back by almost no public land and a lease law that drops away once a hunter pays.

## Indiana's One-Buck Rule: Read This Before You Lease

Before the grades, one rule that changes how every Indiana lease should be written.

**Indiana lets each hunter take one antlered deer per year during the regular deer seasons.** That's across all of them. The DNR's deer questions page says the combined statewide bag limit during the youth, archery, firearms, and muzzleloader seasons is one antlered deer.

What that means in practice:

- **A bow buck ends your buck hunting.** The DNR's seasons page says any deer you harvest counts toward your bag limit for the year, and that limit is one antlered deer across the seasons. Archery opens Oct. 1. Firearms season doesn't open until Nov. 14. A hunter who tags a buck in October has used the year's buck limit before firearms season starts.
- **It's one per hunter, not one per lease.** Every person on your lease has their own limit. If you split a lease with partners or bring guests, each one can take one buck.
- **There are narrow exceptions.** A Deer Reduction Zone allows an additional antlered deer, but only after you've harvested an antlerless deer first. The DNR also names military or refuge hunts and state park deer management hunts as exceptions.
- **Antlerless is separate.** The statewide limit is six antlerless deer, but each county sets its own antlerless limit, and you can't exceed the county's.

Ohio and Minnesota also run one-buck rules. Michigan's true one-buck rule starts in 2027, per our [seven-state regulations roundup](/blog/2026-deer-season-regulations-seven-state-roundup/). For the full Indiana dates and rifle rules, see our [Indiana deer season dates page](/indiana-deer-season-dates/).

**Why this belongs on a lease report card:** If you're paying for the right to hunt a farm, it matters whether you and your partners hold out for a mature buck or fill a tag early. Put your own harvest rules in writing, because the state's rule doesn't decide them for you.

## Buck Quality: A

**How we graded it:** Boone and Crockett's ranking of states by whitetail entries. Top three is an A, fourth through seventh is a B, eighth through twelfth is a C, and anything lower is a D.

Indiana is second. Boone and Crockett's latest ranking, covering entries from January 1, 2022 to December 31, 2024, shows Indiana with 126 entries, one more than Ohio's 125 and nine behind Wisconsin's 135. Iowa is fourth with 107 and Illinois fifth with 105.

The DNR's own harvest numbers point the same way. Its 2024 deer report lists 59,643 antlered deer for the 2024 season, and says that's the highest antlered harvest since reporting began in 1951.

**The fine print:** Record-book entries count the very top of the herd, not the average buck on your lease, and a statewide ranking says nothing about your county. If you want to know why ag-country bucks can look older than they are, see [our Field Note on that](/blog/why-ag-country-bucks-look-older-than-they-are/).

## Deer Numbers: B

**How we graded it:** The latest season's harvest against the three-year average, and where it sits in the record books. Above average and in the top ten seasons ever is an A. Within 10 percent below average is a B. Ten to 25 percent below is a C. Anything worse is a D.

This grade is tentative, and here's why. The DNR lists a 2025 deer report, but we couldn't open it, so the newest season we could read is 2024.

Indiana's DNR reported 126,169 deer for 2024: 59,643 antlered and 66,526 antlerless. That's 4.0 percent above 2023's 121,263. Against the three years before it (112,481 in 2021, 121,854 in 2022, and 121,263 in 2023, an average of about 118,500), 2024 is about 6 percent higher.

It's also the highest total in the DNR's table, which runs from 2015 to 2024. The next highest were 124,668 in 2015 and 124,180 in 2020. We didn't find a ranking of Indiana's biggest seasons ever, so a top-ten finish is unconfirmed. That's why this is a B and not an A.

**The fine print:** Indiana changed its bag limits for 2025-26, replacing the old season antlerless limits and the county bonus quota with a county antlerless limit. That could move the 2025 numbers either way. When we can read the 2025 report, we'll check this grade and update the card. Reported harvest is also a floor, not a count of every deer on the ground.

## Access: D

**How we graded it:** Public land as a share of the state's total land. Twenty percent or more is an A, 10 to 19 percent is a B, 5 to 9 percent is a C, and under 5 percent is a D.

We added up what we could find. The DNR's state lands page (updated July 2026) lists 422,705 acres of DNR-owned land. The Congressional Research Service counts 384,726 acres of federal land in Indiana as of 2018. Together that's about 807,000 acres, or roughly 3.5 percent of the state's 23.2 million acres. An outside article from 2021 puts private ownership at about 96 percent, which lines up.

**The fine print:** Our total leaves out county and city land we couldn't count, so the true number may run a little higher. But not every acre is open to regular deer hunting either. State parks, for example, run special deer management hunts. Either way, it's under 5 percent, and that matches Ohio's D.

There's one more way in. The DNR's Indiana Private Lands Access Program (IPLA) pays private landowners to allow controlled public hunting. Payments run from $3 to $150 per acre depending on which species the landowner allows, and deer is one of the options. The DNR page doesn't say how many acres are enrolled. IPLA hunters can't use trail cameras, trap, or target shoot on enrolled land.

IPLA matters for a leaseholder because it sets a price. A landowner who's weighing your offer can compare it to what the state would pay.

## Herd Health: B-minus

**How we graded it:** CWD and EHD results. An A is no wild CWD and no big EHD losses. A B means CWD is present but confined to a few counties and shows up in about 1 percent or less of tested deer, with a minus for a regional disease hit. A C means CWD is widespread or a region loses deer to EHD year after year. A D is both.

**CWD:** The first Indiana case was found in LaGrange County in April 2024. A second came in February 2025 in Posey County, in the southwest corner of the state. During the 2025-26 season, the DNR confirmed three more positives in wild deer, in Noble, Franklin, and Rush counties, according to its April 20, 2026 announcement. That's at least five known positives in three parts of the state. The DNR's CWD Positive Area now covers Allen, DeKalb, LaGrange, Noble, Steuben, and Whitley counties in the northeast.

We couldn't find a DNR total of positive deer or a testing rate, so we can't check Indiana against our 1 percent line. That's the main reason this grade is tentative.

**EHD:** The DNR's EHD page, updated September 2026, lists reduced antlerless bag limits in eight counties: Bartholomew, Jackson, Jefferson, Jennings, Martin, Scott, Porter, and Switzerland. The page says those reductions remain in place to help offset EHD outbreaks in 2024 and 2025. As of Sept. 15, 2026, the DNR had received only a low number of suspected EHD reports, spread randomly across the state.

**Why B-minus:** CWD has turned up in a handful of counties spread across the northeast, southeast, and southwest, and EHD hit a regional chunk of the state in two straight years. If the DNR's testing shows a rate well above 1 percent, this drops to a C. For the basics, read [our CWD explainer](/blog/chronic-wasting-disease-explained/) and check the DNR's current CWD page before you lease in or near those counties.

## Lease Market and Law: C

**How we graded it:** Two halves. One is Indiana's landowner liability law, and whether it still protects a landowner who accepts a lease payment. The other is cropland cost against the national average, since land cost pushes lease prices up. Our grade averages the two halves. On cropland, within about 15 percent of the national average is an A, 15 to 30 percent over is a B, 30 to 50 percent over is a C, and more than 50 percent over is a D.

**The law drops away when you pay.** Indiana's recreational use statutes, IC 14-22-10-2 and 14-22-10-2.5, say a person who enters land to hunt, with or without permission, has no assurance the premises are safe. They also say the owner doesn't take on liability for injuries caused by other users of the land. But both protections apply only where entry is "without the payment of monetary consideration." The statute defines that as "a fee or other charge for permission to go upon a tract of land."

In plain terms, the protection is for free access. A landowner who charges for access may be held to a higher duty of care toward the paying hunter. Attorney Andrea K. Townsend wrote in a 2017 guide to hunting leases that a paying hunter is likely treated as a business invitee, owed the highest duty of care, and she recommends a well-drafted waiver and liability insurance. That makes Indiana tougher on a paid lease than Ohio, where the shield survives a lease payment, or Michigan, where it narrows. This is our reading of the statute, not legal advice, and we read the statute text in a version we couldn't confirm is the current one, so check the current Indiana Code.

**Cropland is costly, but not Ohio-costly.** USDA's 2026 land values report puts Indiana cropland at $8,600 per acre, up 4.2 percent from 2025. The U.S. average is $6,020, so Indiana runs about 43 percent higher. Ohio is at $10,100 and Michigan at $6,600.

**The fine print:** We couldn't find a published Indiana per-acre hunting lease price. For the ranges we did find across the Corn Belt, see [what hunters actually pay to lease ground](/blog/cost-of-leasing-ag-ground/). Get anything you sign reviewed by a lawyer.

## What the Grades Mean If You Lease in Indiana

- **Write the one-buck rule into your lease.** The state caps you at one buck a year per hunter. Your lease should say who can take a buck, whether there's an age or point standard, and what happens when a guest shoots one.
- **Expect a signed release.** Because the law doesn't shield a landowner who takes your money, expect them to ask for a written release, and for you to carry liability insurance. A written release, an indemnification clause, and proof of the lessee's coverage are the usual safeguards. See [what to look for when leasing hunting land](/blog/leasing-hunting-property-what-to-look-for/).
- **Ask the landowner about IPLA.** The state's per-acre payment is a number they already know. Your offer, and what you'll do on the ground, has to beat it or add something it doesn't.
- **Check the disease maps before you sign.** CWD has been found in the northeast, southeast, and southwest, and eight counties still have lower antlerless limits after EHD.
- **Check the dates before you plan.** Our [Indiana deer season dates page](/indiana-deer-season-dates/) has this year's seasons and rifle rules.

## FAQ: Indiana Deer Hunting Leases

### Is Indiana a good state to lease deer ground?

For buck quality, yes. Indiana ranks second in Boone and Crockett's 2022 to 2024 whitetail entries. But public land is only about 4 percent of the state, so most hunters have to lease or know a landowner. Cropland runs about 43 percent above the national average, and the state's liability law doesn't protect a landowner who charges a lease fee.

### How much does it cost to lease hunting land in Indiana?

We couldn't find a published Indiana per-acre lease price. Our Corn Belt lease cost piece found most decent ag-adjacent leases between $20 and $40 per acre per year, with a wide range around that. Indiana cropland is valued at $8,600 per acre. The state's IPLA program pays landowners $3 to $150 per acre depending on which species they open to hunters.

### Is a landowner protected from liability if they lease land to hunters in Indiana?

Not under the recreational use statutes. Indiana's protection applies only where no fee is charged. A paying hunter is likely treated as a business invitee, which carries a higher duty of care. A written lease, a signed release, and insurance are the usual safeguards. Talk to a lawyer before signing.

### What is Indiana's one-buck rule?

Each hunter may take one antlered deer per year during the regular seasons, across youth, archery, firearms, and muzzleloader. A buck you tag with a bow counts toward that same limit, so it uses your buck tag for the year. A Deer Reduction Zone is the main exception, and you must take an antlerless deer first there.

## The Bottom Line

Indiana gets a C-plus (2.5). That's behind Ohio's B-minus (2.8) and Michigan's B-minus (2.9), but the gap comes from the law and the land, not the deer. Indiana has the second-best bucks in the record book and almost no public ground, and it's the only one of the three where the liability shield disappears the moment you pay. One different call on a grade, like the tentative Deer Numbers, would move it to a B-minus, so don't read the order as a ranking.

If you lease in Indiana, the paperwork matters more than it does in Ohio. Put the one-buck rule, the release, and the insurance in writing before the first sit.
