Iowa has some of the best bucks in the country, some of the priciest farmland in the country, and almost no public ground. That mix is the story of the **Iowa deer hunting lease** market. The report card below puts a grade on each piece.

This is the fourth card in our Ag-Lease Whitetail Report Card series. Every state gets graded on the same five categories with the same ruler, so the grades can be compared. [Ohio went first](/blog/ohio-ag-lease-whitetail-report-card/), then [Michigan](/blog/michigan-ag-lease-whitetail-report-card/) and [Indiana](/blog/indiana-ag-lease-whitetail-report-card/). Iowa is up now.

Every grade rests on a published number, and we show the number. The cutoffs for what counts as an A or a C are ours, and we list them. Where an Iowa number was hard to pin down, we say so.

## Iowa Deer Hunting Lease Report Card: The Grades

**Overall: C.** Big bucks and a steady harvest, held back by almost no public land, the highest cropland prices in the Corn Belt, and a liability law that drops away once a hunter pays.

## Iowa's New Zone A/B Rules: Read This Before You Lease

Before the grades, a change that matters for where you lease.

**For 2026, Iowa split resident deer hunting into Zone A and Zone B.** Zone B covers eight northwest counties: Crawford, Ida, Lyon, O'Brien, Plymouth, Shelby, Sioux, and Woodbury. DNR set it up because deer there are down more than 50 percent from 15 to 20 years ago, which it ties to limited habitat and repeated EHD outbreaks.

What that means in practice:

- **Zone B is antlered-buck-only** in nearly every season. The exceptions are the Youth, Disabled, Landowner-Tenant, and Depredation seasons.
- **No party hunting in Zone B.** You can't fill a partner's tag.
- **A small number of county-specific Any-Deer licenses** go on sale Aug. 15, first come, first served. For most hunters, that's the only way to shoot a doe in Zone B. Zone licenses are tied to their zone, so a Zone A license won't work there.
- **Zone A is mostly unchanged,** with one tweak. Audubon, Carroll, and Greene counties are now antlered-buck-only during first regular gun season.

Nonresidents face a different hurdle. Iowa caps nonresident deer tags and hands them out by lottery. DNR's 2025-26 report says 6,055 nonresident any-deer licenses were issued, and 45 percent of those hunters filled one. Our [Iowa deer season dates page](/iowa-deer-season-dates/) has the application window and costs.

**Why this belongs on a lease report card:** A lease in northwest Iowa now comes with rules that a lease in Zone A doesn't. If your plan is to manage the herd by taking does, check which zone the farm sits in before you pay a dime. And if you're from out of state, a signed lease doesn't get you a tag.

## Buck Quality: B

**How we graded it:** Boone and Crockett's ranking of states by whitetail entries. Top three is an A, fourth through seventh is a B, eighth through twelfth is a C, and anything lower is a D.

Iowa is fourth. Boone and Crockett's latest ranking, covering entries from January 1, 2022 to December 31, 2024, puts Wisconsin first with 135, Indiana second with 126, and Ohio third with 125. Iowa has 107, and Illinois is right behind with 105.

Iowa is climbing. In Boone and Crockett's 2020 to 2022 window, it sat fifth with 83 entries. It still ranks third in the all-time books, so the record is deep, but the three states ahead of it are the ones producing right now.

**The fine print:** Record-book entries count the very top of the herd, not the average buck on your lease. A statewide rank says nothing about your county. If you want to know why ag-country bucks can look older than they are, see [our Field Note on that](/blog/why-ag-country-bucks-look-older-than-they-are/).

## Deer Numbers: B

**How we graded it:** The latest season's harvest against the three-year average. Above average and in the top ten seasons ever is an A. Within 10 percent below average is a B. Ten to 25 percent below is a C. Anything worse is a D.

Iowa's DNR reported 101,919 deer for the 2025-26 season. That's up 1 percent from 2024-25. Against the three seasons before it (109,600 in 2022-23, 104,274 in 2023-24, and 101,282 in 2024-25, an average of about 105,000), the 2025-26 total is about 3 percent lower. That lands in B territory.

The state split matters more than the total. As the 2025 seasons ran, DNR's state deer biologist said a strong harvest in eastern Iowa was making up for a lower harvest in the western, central, southern, and northwestern regions. In plain terms, the east is carrying the numbers. The DNR's bowhunter survey, a trend index, showed deer sightings down 3 percent in 2025.

**The fine print:** Reported harvest is a floor, not a count of every deer on the ground. DNR's own estimate of total hunting deaths, which adds wounding loss and hunters who don't report, is 153,239 deer. We also don't have a ranking of Iowa's biggest seasons, so we can't say whether this one is top ten. That's one more reason it stays a B and not an A.

## Access: D

**How we graded it:** Public land as a share of the state's total land. Twenty percent or more is an A, 10 to 19 percent is a B, 5 to 9 percent is a C, and under 5 percent is a D.

Iowa is one of the toughest states in the country for public ground. The state is about 36 million acres. DNR manages roughly 390,000 to 425,000 acres of it, depending on the source. Add county, federal, and private land open through the state's access program, and a guest column in The Gazette put the total at about 650,000 acres of public hunting ground, which it says is well under 2 percent of the state.

**The fine print:** Not every public acre is open for deer. Some is refuge, and some is state park. That's why we say "well under 2 percent" and not a tighter number. Either way it's far below the 5 percent line, and Iowa has less public ground than Ohio or Indiana.

There's one more way in. The Iowa Habitat and Access Program, or IHAP, gives landowners money and habitat help in return for opening their ground to public hunting. Tracts must be at least 40 acres, are enrolled for three to 10 years, and are open September 1 through May 31. The newest enrollment figure we found was from 2021: 29,031 acres on 184 tracts. We couldn't find a newer count.

IHAP matters to a leaseholder because it sets a comparison. A landowner weighing your offer can see what the state will help pay for if he opens the gate to everyone.

## Herd Health: C

**How we graded it:** CWD and EHD results. An A is no wild CWD and no big EHD losses. A B means CWD is present but confined to a few counties and shows up in about 1 percent or less of tested deer, with a minus for a regional disease hit. A C means CWD is widespread or a region loses deer to EHD year after year. A D is both.

**CWD:** DNR's 2025-26 report counts 192 new positive deer. Ten counties were added to the list: Buchanan, Butler, Crawford, Delaware, Linn, Marion, Mills, Plymouth, Scott, and Warren. That brings the total to 39 of Iowa's 99 counties. The first positive was in Allamakee County in 2013. DNR says it has tested more than 110,000 wild deer since 2002.

The report doesn't give a positive rate for the 2025-26 season, so we can't check Iowa against our 1 percent line. But 39 counties across the northeast, south-central, and now the west is not confined to a few spots. That's why it lands at a C.

**EHD:** DNR calls 2023 and 2024 two of the most severe hemorrhagic disease outbreaks in state history. 2025 was the opposite: fewer than 50 suspected cases statewide, which DNR called exceptionally mild. One good year doesn't erase two bad ones, and it's one of the reasons the western herd is where it is. We didn't find a county-by-county loss figure.

For the basics, read [our CWD explainer](/blog/chronic-wasting-disease-explained/) and check the DNR's CWD map for the county you want to lease in.

## Lease Market and Law: D

**How we graded it:** Two halves. One is Iowa's landowner liability law, and whether it still protects a landowner who accepts a lease payment. The other is cropland cost against the national average, since land cost pushes lease prices up. Our grade averages the two halves. On cropland, within about 15 percent of the national average is an A, 15 to 30 percent over is a B, 30 to 50 percent over is a C, and more than 50 percent over is a D.

**The law drops away when you pay.** Iowa Code chapter 461C is the state's recreational use law. Its stated purpose is to get landowners to open their land by limiting their liability. It says a landowner who lets someone use the land for recreation, which includes hunting, doesn't owe a duty to keep it safe. But that protection applies where the landowner invites or permits use "without charge." Section 461C.6 says the chapter doesn't limit liability for injuries when the landowner charges the person who comes on the land.

The law does carve out one kind of payment. If the land is leased to the federal government or the state, the money the owner gets for that doesn't count as a charge. A hunter's lease check isn't covered by that carve-out.

In plain terms, Iowa's shield is for free access. A landowner who charges for access may be open to a claim the law would otherwise block. That puts Iowa with Indiana and well behind Ohio, where the shield survives a lease payment. That's our reading of the statute, not legal advice. The chapter has been amended over the years, so check the current Iowa Code before you sign anything.

**Cropland is the most expensive in the Corn Belt.** USDA's 2026 land values report puts Iowa cropland at $10,700 per acre, up 3.9 percent from 2025. The U.S. average is $6,020, so Iowa runs about 78 percent higher. Illinois is at $10,200, Ohio at $10,100, and Indiana at $8,600. When a farmer's ground is worth that much, a hunting check has to compete with the crop.

**The fine print:** We couldn't find a published Iowa per-acre hunting lease price. Our [Corn Belt lease cost piece](/blog/cost-of-leasing-ag-ground/) found some hot counties in Illinois and Iowa reportedly bidding well past the usual range. Get anything you sign reviewed by a lawyer.

## What the Grades Mean If You Lease in Iowa

- **Check the zone before you sign.** Zone B in the northwest is antlered-buck-only with no party hunting. Zone A is mostly regular rules.
- **Expect a signed release.** Because the law doesn't shield a landowner who charges, expect to be asked for a written release and proof of liability insurance. See [what to look for when leasing hunting land](/blog/leasing-hunting-property-what-to-look-for/).
- **Out-of-state? Get the tag first.** A nonresident can sign a lease and still lose the tag lottery. Know the application window before you pay.
- **Ask the landowner about IHAP.** The state's offer is a number he already knows. Your offer has to beat it or add something it doesn't.
- **Ask DNR for county numbers.** The statewide harvest hides a strong east and a weak west, so the farm's county tells you more than the state total.
- **Check the dates before you plan.** Our [Iowa deer season dates page](/iowa-deer-season-dates/) has this year's seasons and rifle rules, and our [Iowa soybean harvest timeline](/blog/iowa-soybean-harvest-timeline/) shows when the beans come off.

## FAQ: Iowa Deer Hunting Leases

### Is Iowa a good state to lease deer ground?

For bucks, yes. Iowa ranks fourth in Boone and Crockett's 2022 to 2024 whitetail entries. But public land is well under 2 percent of the state, cropland is the priciest in the Corn Belt at $10,700 per acre, and the state's liability law doesn't protect a landowner who charges a lease fee.

### How much does it cost to lease hunting land in Iowa?

We couldn't find a published Iowa per-acre lease price. Our Corn Belt lease cost piece found most decent ag-adjacent leases between $20 and $40 per acre per year, with some hot Iowa and Illinois counties bidding higher. The state's IHAP program gives landowners funding and habitat help for opening ground to public hunters.

### Is a landowner protected from liability if they lease land to hunters in Iowa?

Not under the recreational use law. Iowa Code 461C limits a landowner's liability for people he lets on the land without charge, and section 461C.6 says that doesn't cover injuries when the landowner charges for access. A written lease, a signed release, and insurance are the usual safeguards. Talk to a lawyer before signing.

### What are Iowa's Zone A and Zone B?

Starting in 2026, Iowa split resident deer hunting into two zones. Zone B covers eight northwest counties and is antlered-buck-only in most seasons, with no party hunting. Zone A is the rest of the state and is mostly unchanged.

## The Bottom Line

Iowa gets a C (2.0). That's behind Ohio's B-minus (2.8), Michigan's B-minus (2.9), and Indiana's C-plus (2.5). The deer aren't the problem. Iowa has the fourth-best bucks in the record book and a harvest that held up through two rough disease years. The problem is the ground: almost none of it is public, it costs more than anywhere else in the Corn Belt, and the law stops protecting the landowner the moment you pay.

If you lease in Iowa, do the paperwork first and the scouting second. Check the zone, get the release and the insurance in writing, and don't count on a tag you haven't drawn.
