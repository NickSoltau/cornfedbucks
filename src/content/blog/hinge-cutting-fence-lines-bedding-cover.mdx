On a lot of Midwest ground, the only real cover for a mile is the fence line. A strip of box elder, hackberry, and brush between two crop fields might be 30 feet wide. That's where the deer bed, because there's nowhere else to go. So if you lease that ground, hinge cutting the fence line for bedding cover is one of the few habitat jobs that fits.

It's also one of the easiest jobs to get wrong. Here's what hinge cutting actually is, what the research does and doesn't say, and what to settle with the landowner before you pick up a saw.

## What Hinge Cutting Is

You cut a small tree about halfway through and push it over. It stays attached to the stump by a strip of wood and bark, so it keeps living. Think of a door hinge. The door swings down, but it's still bolted to the frame.

The tree keeps its leaves and sends up new shoots, and it lies sideways at deer height. That gives you cover right away, without waiting years for brush to grow. University of Georgia wildlife specialist Jeff Jackson describes it as cutting the tree halfway through so it falls and creates instant cover but doesn't die.

## Does Hinge Cutting Actually Work? What the Study Found

Here's the best research we found. Dr. Craig Harper and his students at the University of Tennessee set up three study sites in Mississippi, Pennsylvania, and Tennessee. Each site had a 10-acre hardwood block: five acres treated and five acres left alone as a comparison. They hinge-cut the small mid-story trees in winter 2023. They also treated larger trees with herbicide to open the canopy to at least 50 percent sunlight, then watched with eight trail cameras per site.

The results, reported by the National Deer Association, were strong. Average daily deer detections in the treated blocks went from 5.1 to 9.0, a 76 percent jump. During hunting season, detections in treated areas were up 78 percent, while the untreated comparison areas dropped 24 percent.

Three cautions. First, the treatment was more than hinge cutting, because the canopy work with herbicide was part of it. Second, this was preliminary data from three sites, and the cameras are still running. Third, those were five-acre blocks of woods. A fence line is a skinny strip. Nobody has told us the same numbers would hold on a 30-foot hedgerow in Ohio or Indiana. The direction is encouraging, but don't count on the exact numbers.

## Does Your Fence Line Even Need Hinge Cutting?

Sometimes. If the line is already thick with brush down at deer height, leave it alone. Hinge cutting makes sense when the line is tall, open underneath, and has little low cover. Wind blows through it, deer can see across it, and they don't want to bed there.

## Ask Before You Cut

You're leasing. The trees aren't yours, and cutting them can break your lease.

- **Get permission in writing.** A text or email from the landowner is better than a handshake.
- **Ask about timber value.** A forester quoted by the National Deer Association, A.J. Smith, has seen people drop a 10- to 14-inch sugar maple worth a few hundred to a thousand dollars. If you can't name the species you're cutting, don't cut it.
- **Ask whether the fence matters.** A tree that falls on a livestock fence or a neighbor's wire is a bad day for everybody.

For more on what to ask before you sign, see [What to Look for When Leasing Hunting Land](/blog/leasing-hunting-property-what-to-look-for/) and [What Hunters Actually Pay to Lease Corn Belt Ground](/blog/cost-of-leasing-ag-ground/).

## How to Hinge Cut a Fence Line the Right Way

The size, saw work, and direction all matter.

- **Stay small.** The National Deer Association's own writer says a hinge cut on the wrong tree "greatly magnifies the risk that you will be injured." Two hinge-cut fans quoted there put the limit at 8 inches across. One of them does most of his cutting with a hand saw on trees under 4 inches. Use a regular felling cut on anything bigger.
- **Cut about halfway through.** Jackson usually cuts at waist level because it's easier. On thin stems, he says, two or three shallow cuts side by side bend better than one deep one.
- **Push it down.** Force the stem over until it's lying parallel to the ground. The Tennessee team calls a push pole critical for leaning the tree while cutting through as little of the stem as possible.
- **Watch the lean.** Don't let a tree fall toward the cut side. It's more likely to break.
- **Respect the kickback.** Jackson warns that most chainsaw kickback happens when the nose of the bar touches wood, especially during plunge cuts.
- **Put them in a row.** Biologist Zack Vucurevich calls trees hinge cut in a row, all hinging the same direction, a "closed edge," and uses them to direct deer movement.

## Don't Overdo It

More isn't better. Vucurevich warns that piling up horizontal structure can condition deer to loiter in that spot, and that may not be where you want them. He says a bad cut can be a 10- to 15-year commitment before the evidence is gone. His rule for bedding thickets: of the trees you cut, no more than 25 percent should be hinged.

Watch for invasives, too. Vucurevich lists bittersweet, bush honeysuckle, and multiflora rose among the plants that move into disturbed ground.

## Leave the Oaks Standing

Our rule: if you have a choice, don't cut an oak. Acorns are a staple fall food for deer, and a mast-producing oak is worth more standing than lying on its side. That said, the National Deer Association's tree guide says oaks are good for both mast and hinge cutting. It says that in an area with plenty of oaks you can leave a few for acorns and hinge the rest. A thin fence line may not have oaks to spare, so we'd leave them all. Hinge a lower-value tree next to them instead, once you've checked timber value with the landowner.

## When to Do It

The Tennessee study cut its trees in winter. That's the timing we'd plan around. It also means October is for scouting, not cutting. Walk the line now while the deer are using it, mark the spots, ask the landowner, and plan the work for the dormant season. We couldn't find a Corn Belt-specific rule on the best cutting dates, so ask your state forester or DNR.

## FAQ: Hinge-Cutting Fence Lines

### What is hinge cutting?

You cut a small tree about halfway through and push it over. The tree stays attached to the stump and keeps growing, so it makes cover at deer height right away.

### Does hinge cutting really bring deer in?

A University of Tennessee study found deer detections up 76 percent in treated blocks. It's early data from three sites using five-acre woodlots, and the treatment also included canopy work with herbicide, so it may not carry over exactly to a thin fence line.

### What size tree can you hinge cut?

Stick to small trees. Two hinge-cut fans quoted by the National Deer Association put the limit at 8 inches across, and one mostly cuts trees under 4 inches with a hand saw. Use a standard felling cut on anything bigger.

### Can I hinge cut on leased land?

Only with the landowner's permission, and it's smart to get it in writing. Ask about timber value and fences first.

### When is the best time to hinge cut?

The Tennessee study cut in winter, so that's the timing to plan around. Ask your state forestry office or DNR for the best window in your area.

### Should I hinge cut oak trees?

If you have a choice, no. Oaks produce acorns, a favorite fall deer food, so they're worth more standing. Hinge cut the less valuable trees around them.

## The Bottom Line

Hinge cutting can turn a thin, windy fence line into real bedding cover, but it works best when you keep the trees small, the cuts few, and the landowner in the loop. Scout it this fall, get your permission, and plan the saw work for winter.
