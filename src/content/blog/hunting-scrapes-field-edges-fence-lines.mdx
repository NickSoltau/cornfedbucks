You find a fresh scrape on a field corner. The dirt is wet, the branch above it is chewed, and the hair on your neck stands up. You hang a stand right over it and sit all morning. Nothing shows.

That's not bad luck. It's how **scrape hunting** works. Research says most scrape visits happen after dark, so the scrape is less a place to sit than a clue about where bucks walk. This Field Note covers what the studies found, where scrapes show up on ag ground, and whether a mock scrape is worth the trouble.

One honest note up front. We didn't find a study that looked at scrapes on Midwest row-crop fence lines specifically. What we have is solid general research, plus how our own fence-line reading fits it. We'll mark which is which.

## What a Scrape Actually Is

A scrape is a buck's message board. He paws a patch of bare dirt under a low, overhanging branch, rubs and chews the branch, and often urinates in the dirt. That branch is the licking branch, and it's the part that matters most.

Does and other bucks stop by to read the news. The National Deer Association says scrapes are mostly made by bucks, but does visit too, and sometimes leave their own scent. Many visitors just check the scent and leave without adding any.

That means a scrape tells you a buck lives nearby and that other deer know it. It doesn't tell you he's standing there at 7 a.m.

## Scrape Hunting Timing: Mostly After Dark

This is the finding that changes where you sit.

University of Georgia researcher Karen Alexy watched scrapes with motion-triggered cameras for two years. About 85 percent of the visits she recorded came after dark, as reported in [Outdoor Life](https://www.outdoorlife.com/when-bucks-use-scrapes-new-research-reveals-startling-facts-about-buck-activity) and [American Hunter](https://americanhunter.org/content/most-scrapes-aren-t-worth-a-sit). Karl Miller, who guided the study, has put it at "almost 90%." The Outdoor Life report adds that few of the mature bucks (3½ years and older) shot on the property were ever recorded at the scrapes, even though some were killed within a few hundred yards of them. The National Deer Association's guide to rubs and scrapes puts it at "over 80%" of scrape use at night.

**When the season matters.** Scraping peaks in the pre-rut and then drops. A Mississippi State University study found visits peaked before the rut and fell sharply after. University of Georgia deer biologist Karl Miller says peak scraping comes about two weeks before peak breeding.

Now do the math with our own state numbers. In our [rut timing guide](/blog/when-does-the-rut-start/), Ohio's peak breeding window is Nov. 11 to 17, and Iowa's is the first half of November. Two weeks ahead of that puts the best scrape activity in late October and early November. That's this week and next.

**The fine print:** Alexy's study was in Georgia, where the rut runs on a different calendar. Alexy also found a smaller second burst of scrape checking from about Nov. 19 to Dec. 9, especially by bucks 2½ and older. The pattern carries over. The exact dates don't, so use your state's own numbers.

## Where Scrapes Show Up on Ag Ground

Scrapes follow edges. The National Deer Association says they're concentrated "where two or more vegetation types or age classes meet." Field edges, trails, and the inside of the woods all hold them. In the Outdoor Life account of Alexy's study, four of the six scrapes she monitored were along field edges.

On row-crop ground, that points to a few spots:

- **The woodlot edge where it meets a field.** Cover on one side, crop on the other.
- **The corner of a field where two cover types meet.** Think CRP meeting a hedgerow.
- **A fence line with real trees on it.** A bare wire fence between two bean fields is unlikely to hold one.

That last one is our read, not a study result, but it follows from how scrapes work. The National Deer Association says an overhanging branch at the right height usually decides the exact spot. A buck needs a licking branch. A fence line with no trees has nothing to lick.

That's why the fence lines worth checking look like the ones in our [fence-line reading guide](/blog/reading-a-fence-line/): old lines grown up with locust, or an Osage orange hedgerow. Those have the branches. If you're also reading where deer cross, that guide covers it.

## How to Hunt a Scrape Without Sitting on It

If bucks mostly hit scrapes in the dark, you want to be where they walk on the way there, not on top of it.

- **Set up between bedding and the scrape or feed.** The National Deer Association suggests this over sitting directly on the scrape. Outdoor Life's write-up of Alexy's study says to find the trail between bedding cover and a food source, then set up 100 to 200 yards from the feed, toward the bedding.
- **Hunt the late-afternoon pre-rut.** That's when a buck leaving bed ahead of dark is most likely to be inside bow or rifle range of that trail.
- **Stay downwind, and stay back.** American Hunter's roundup of the research suggests at least 20 yards downwind, since deer may check a scrape's scent without walking into it.
- **Look for last year's scrapes.** Deer reopened 16 of the 20 scrapes that retired Michigan DNR researcher John Ozoga monitored, and about 75 percent of Alexy's. A scrape that's been used before is a good bet to be used again.

For stand height, wind, and your walk in on an open fence line, see our [fence-line stand setups](/blog/fence-line-stand-setups/).

**Don't wear it out.** A scrape you walk to every few days is a scrape bucks start skipping. A trail camera can tell you what's using it without you. Our [trail camera strategy guide](/blog/trail-camera-cell-cam-strategy/) covers cell cams versus card cams, and which camera makes sense hung right on a scrape.

## Mock Scrapes: Do They Fool Bucks?

A mock scrape is one you make yourself: bare dirt, a licking branch, and often scent. The newest research on whether they work came out of the Mississippi State University Deer Lab, and the National Deer Association shared the results in September.

The researchers worked on a University of Tennessee property in west Tennessee that has a hunting club and a farming operation. They watched 25 mock scrapes and 27 natural ones, across field edges, maintenance roads, and timber, with a trail camera on every one. The mock scrapes had a licking branch and a dripper of synthetic urine. The researchers counted only the visits where a deer actually touched the scrape.

Here's what they found:

- **Natural scrapes got more than three times the action.** Deer interacted with natural scrapes 824 times and with mock scrapes 232 times.
- **Deer used natural scrapes differently.** They were 25 times more likely to rub-urinate at a natural scrape, and seven times more likely to work the licking branch. At mock scrapes, deer mostly just investigated.
- **Bucks came back to the real ones.** Of 167 bucks tracked, 91 returned to a natural scrape at least twice. Only 10 returned to a mock one, and the most any single buck visited a mock scrape was three times.
- **Mature bucks favored natural scrapes.** Bucks 3½ and older used natural scrapes more than young bucks did. At mock scrapes, use was about equal across age classes.

The researchers' read was that deer "appear to know the difference." But they also said mock scrapes can still pull a buck in to take a look, especially one downwind.

**Our verdict:** A mock scrape won't fool a buck the way a real one does, and it's not a reason to expect return visits. It's a way to get a buck to stop and investigate at a spot you picked. That can be worth something if the spot is a good one for your stand, and not much if it isn't. This is a study of one property in Tennessee, so treat the numbers as a direction, not a promise.

## If You Make One

If you want to try it, put the work into the spot, not the supplies.

- **Pick a spot that already works.** Use a place with other sign, near a trail or edge where bucks already travel, and downwind of your stand location.
- **Use a real licking branch.** The study's mock scrapes had one. Without a branch to work, you're just digging a hole.
- **Check your state's rules on scents.** Some states restrict natural deer urine products because of CWD, and rules change. Check your DNR before you buy anything.
- **Ask before you dig on leased ground.** A scrape is small, but a field edge is a farmer's working ground. A quick call beats a surprise.
- **Run a camera on it.** It's the easiest way to find out whether it's drawing anything.

## FAQ: Hunting Scrapes

### Do bucks use scrapes during the day?

Some do, but most scrape use is at night. Research from the University of Georgia found 85 to 90 percent of recorded visits were after dark, and the National Deer Association reports over 80 percent. Daylight visits happen, mostly near dawn and dusk.

### When is the best time to hunt scrapes?

The pre-rut. Karl Miller of the University of Georgia says peak scraping comes about two weeks before peak breeding. In Ohio, where peak breeding is Nov. 11 to 17, that puts the best scrape activity in late October and early November.

### Do mock scrapes work?

Partly. A Mississippi State University Deer Lab study found deer interacted with natural scrapes more than three times as often as mock ones, and far fewer bucks came back to mock scrapes. Mock scrapes can still draw a buck in to investigate, especially from downwind.

### Why are scientists studying scrapes for CWD?

Scrapes are places where deer touch the same ground and branches, so they may spread disease. Researchers in Tennessee found evidence of prion contamination at scrape sites, and they suggest scrapes could be used as early warning spots for CWD surveillance.

## The Bottom Line

A scrape tells you where a buck walks, not where he'll be at sunrise. Hunt the trail between bedding and the edge, hunt the pre-rut window that's opening now, and treat a mock scrape as a way to pull a buck close, not a trick that fools him.
