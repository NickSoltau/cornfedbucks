Ohio grows big deer, plenty of them, and almost none of the ground is public. That's the whole story of the **Ohio deer hunting lease** market in one sentence. The report card below puts a grade on each piece of it, so you can see where Ohio is strong and where a leaseholder will feel the squeeze.

This is the first in our Ag-Lease Whitetail Report Card series. Each state gets graded on the same five categories with the same ruler, so the grades can be compared from state to state. Ohio goes first. Michigan is next.

Every grade rests on a published number, and we show the number. The cutoffs for what counts as an A or a C are ours, and we list them. If you'd draw the line somewhere else, you can see exactly where we drew ours.

## Ohio Deer Hunting Lease Report Card: The Grades

**Overall: B-minus.** Great deer and good lease law, held back by almost no public land and expensive farmland.

## Buck Quality: A

**How we graded it:** Boone and Crockett's ranking of states by whitetail entries. Top three is an A, fourth through seventh is a B, eighth through twelfth is a C, and anything lower is a D.

Ohio is third. Boone and Crockett's latest ranking, covering entries from January 1, 2022 to December 31, 2024, shows Ohio with 125 entries, up from fifth in the all-time records through 2019. Boone and Crockett also says Ohio's big bucks aren't "concentrated in a handful of hotspot counties." That's good news if your lease isn't in Coshocton.

Ohio's own age data backs it up. In the 2024-25 season, nearly one-third of antlered deer taken during gun season were 3.5 years or older, according to ODNR's annual deer summary. In the archery sample, it was 39.2 percent.

**The fine print:** Boone and Crockett counts record-book entries. It doesn't tell you how many of those came off row-crop ground. It's also a count of bucks entered, not a measure of how many hunters are chasing them. If you want to know why ag-country bucks can look older than they are, see [our Field Note on that](/blog/why-ag-country-bucks-look-older-than-they-are/).

## Deer Numbers: A

**How we graded it:** The latest season's harvest against the three-year average, and where it sits in the record books. Above average and in the top ten seasons ever is an A. Within 10 percent below average is a B. Ten to 25 percent below is a C. Anything worse is a D.

Ohio hunters checked 232,142 deer in 2025-26, according to ODNR. That's about 5 percent above the 2022–2024 average of 221,013, and the seventh-highest total on record. It's the fourth season in a row over 200,000. It was down about 2.5 percent from 2024-25, which came in at 238,137.

The northwest corner, where a lot of Ohio's row-crop leases sit, ran hotter than the state. Local reporting of ODNR's county numbers shows these six counties, with the three-year average in parentheses:

- Williams: 2,842 (2,217)
- Defiance: 1,887 (1,942)
- Fulton: 1,489 (1,099)
- Putnam: 1,376 (1,200)
- Paulding: 1,286 (1,311)
- Henry: 1,181 (1,042)

Add them up and the six counties checked 10,061 deer against an average of 8,811, about 14 percent more. That math is ours, not ODNR's.

There's a landowner angle too. In ODNR's 2023 goal-setting survey, 46 percent of the 3,000 landowners surveyed said they had too many deer on their farms. Deer damage control permits climbed 57 percent over the three-year average in 2024-25. A farmer who's losing beans to deer is often a farmer who'll talk to a hunter.

**The fine print:** ODNR says only 21 of Ohio's 88 counties killed enough antlerless deer in 2024-25 to hold the herd steady. In most counties, deer numbers are still growing, and ODNR says that's already beyond what many people will tolerate in parts of the state. That can mean tighter bag limits down the road. See our [Ohio deer season dates page](/ohio-deer-season-dates/) for this year's limits.

## Access: D

**How we graded it:** Public land as a share of the state's total land. Twenty percent or more is an A, 10 to 19 percent is a B, 5 to 9 percent is a C, and under 5 percent is a D.

ODNR's annual summary puts public land at roughly 4 percent of Ohio. That's the lowest grade on the card, and it's the reason lease hunting exists here. If you want to hunt Ohio, you're mostly hunting someone's farm.

And a lot of those farms already have a hunter on them. Landowners checked 62,933 deer on their own property without a permit in 2025-26, about 27 percent of the statewide harvest by our math. Hunters bought 424,241 deer permits that season, and out-of-state hunters from every state came to Ohio. The top five states were Pennsylvania, Michigan, North Carolina, New York, and West Virginia.

**The fine print:** A D here doesn't mean Ohio is bad hunting. It means the ground is hard to find and hard to hold. It's also where an ag-lease site has to be honest: the grade is about finding ground, not about what's on it. For what to check on a parcel, see [what to look for when leasing hunting land](/blog/leasing-hunting-property-what-to-look-for/).

## Herd Health: B-minus

**How we graded it:** CWD and EHD results. An A is no wild CWD and no big EHD losses. A B means CWD is present but confined to a few counties and shows up in about 1 percent or less of tested deer, with a minus for a regional disease hit. A C means CWD is widespread or a region loses deer to EHD year after year. A D is both.

**CWD:** Ohio's first two wild CWD cases came in late 2020 and early 2021 in Wyandot County, ODNR says. As of June 2026, 115 deer in Ohio have tested positive. Of the 6,617 deer tested during 2025-26, 40 were positive, or about 0.6 percent by our math. The positives came from Allen, Crawford, Hancock, Hardin, Marion, and Wyandot counties. The disease surveillance area covers all of Allen, Hardin, Marion, and Wyandot counties, plus parts of five more.

**EHD:** An outbreak of epizootic hemorrhagic disease hit Athens, Meigs, Morgan, and Washington counties in southeast Ohio in the summer of 2025. Those four counties checked 4,289 deer in 2025-26, down from 15,659 the year before. That's a drop of about 73 percent. Athens, Meigs, and Washington have lower bag limits for 2026-27.

**The fine print:** The 0.6 percent number is the share of deer that were tested, not the share of the whole herd. Check [our CWD explainer](/blog/chronic-wasting-disease-explained/) before you hunt inside the surveillance area.

## Lease Market and Law: C-plus

**How we graded it:** Two halves. One is Ohio's landowner liability law, and whether it still protects a landowner who accepts a lease payment. The other is cropland cost against the national average, since land cost pushes lease prices up. Strong law and cropland more than 50 percent over the national average works out to a C-plus.

**The law is good for leasing.** Ohio Revised Code 1533.181 says an owner, lessee, or occupant of private land owes no duty to keep the premises safe for a "recreational user." The definition of recreational user in ORC 1533.18 includes someone with permission to hunt, and it says a lease payment to a private landowner doesn't take that person out of the definition. In plain terms, a landowner who collects a hunting lease payment is still covered. OSU Extension adds that the protection won't cover a landowner who willfully or recklessly causes harm, and gives the example of letting too many hunters on and not managing them.

**The land is expensive.** USDA's 2026 land values report puts Ohio cropland at $10,100 per acre, up 3.6 percent from 2025. The U.S. average is $6,020. Ohio is about 68 percent higher, by our math. When a landowner's ground is worth that much, you're competing with the crop, and with other hunters, for the right to be there.

**The fine print:** We couldn't find any published Ohio hunting lease price per acre, so this grade doesn't use one. For the ranges we did find across the Corn Belt, see [what hunters actually pay to lease ground](/blog/cost-of-leasing-ag-ground/). The law above isn't legal advice. Get anything you sign reviewed by a lawyer.

## What the Grades Mean If You Lease in Ohio

- **Expect to work for the ground, not the deer.** Ohio's two A's are the herd. The D and the C-plus are about getting onto land and keeping it.
- **Start in the northwest if you hunt row crops.** Those counties ran above their averages and sit far from the EHD counties. They're also nearer CWD ones, so check the surveillance area map.
- **Talk to farmers losing crops to deer.** Ohio's deer damage permits are up sharply. That's a way in.
- **Get it in writing.** Ohio's liability law helps a landowner, but a lease should still spell out who hunts, where, and when. For timing your sits around the combine, see [Ohio's two-stage corn harvest guide](/blog/ohio-two-stage-corn-harvest-deer-hunting/).

## FAQ: Ohio Deer Hunting Leases

### Is Ohio a good state to lease deer ground?

For the deer, yes. Ohio ranks third among states for Boone and Crockett whitetail entries from 2022 to 2024, and hunters checked 232,142 deer in 2025-26. The hard part is access. Public land is only about 4 percent of the state, so most hunters end up on private ground.

### How much does it cost to lease hunting land in Ohio?

We couldn't find a published Ohio-specific price per acre. Our Corn Belt lease cost piece found most decent ag-adjacent leases between $20 and $40 per acre per year, with a wide range around that. Ohio cropland is valued at $10,100 per acre, well above the U.S. average, so local prices may run higher in some counties.

### Is a landowner protected from liability if they lease land to hunters in Ohio?

Ohio's recreational user statute, ORC 1533.181, says owners, lessees, and occupants of private land owe no duty to keep the premises safe for a recreational user. The definition in ORC 1533.18 doesn't exclude a lease payment to a private landowner. The protection has limits, including willful or reckless conduct. Talk to a lawyer before signing a lease.

### Does CWD affect hunting leases in Ohio?

It can, mostly in the northwest and west-central part of the state. Ohio's disease surveillance area covers all of Allen, Hardin, Marion, and Wyandot counties and parts of five others, and hunting rules there differ from the rest of the state. Check ODNR's current CWD page before you lease inside that area.

## The Bottom Line

Ohio gets a B-minus. The deer are an A, the access is a D, and the lease market lands in between. If you can find ground, it's some of the best whitetail country in the Corn Belt. Finding it is the work.
