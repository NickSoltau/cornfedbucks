**Overall: B-.** The top bucks in the nation and a harvest at a 13-year high, held back by CWD that has spread across the state and a liability law that stops protecting a landowner once lease income passes $2,000 a year.

## Before You Lease in Wisconsin: Authorizations, Antlerless Tags, and CWD Rules

Before the grades, a few Wisconsin rules that change what a lease is worth.

- **No one-buck rule, and buck tags don't carry across seasons.** The bag limit is one buck per unused buck harvest authorization, and the authorizations are season- and weapon-specific. A bow buck tag filled in October doesn't give you a gun-season buck in November. There's no statewide antler point restriction. This year's nine-day gun season runs Nov. 21 to 29.
- **Antlerless deer follow the zone.** An antlerless authorization is valid for a specific zone, unit, or land type, not the whole state. DNR also runs an antlerless-only holiday hunt, Dec. 24 through Jan. 1, in select Farmland Zone counties.
- **Baiting and feeding are banned in many counties.** DNR says it's illegal to place, use, or hunt deer over bait or feed in many counties. State law requires a 3-year ban in a county with a CWD detection and a 2-year ban in adjoining counties within 10 miles, so the list keeps changing. Carcass-transport limits apply too. Check DNR's baiting map and carcass-movement page for the county you plan to hunt.
- **Rifles are legal under state rules, but local rules can stack on top.** Our [Wisconsin deer season dates page](/wisconsin-deer-season-dates/) lists the dates, licenses, and the state caliber rule. Towns, villages, and counties can add their own limits by ordinance, so call the town clerk before you count on a rifle. Our [straight-wall cartridge guide](/blog/straight-wall-cartridge-state-regulations/) covers how Wisconsin compares to its neighbors.

**Why this belongs on a lease report card:** Two farms in neighboring counties can have different antlerless options, different baiting rules, and different local rifle rules. Look up the county before you pay a dime.

## Buck Quality: A

**How we graded it:** Boone and Crockett's ranking of states by whitetail entries. Top three is an A, fourth through seventh is a B, eighth through twelfth is a C, and anything lower is a D.

Wisconsin is first. Boone and Crockett's latest ranking, covering entries from January 1, 2022 to December 31, 2024, puts Wisconsin at 135, nine ahead of Indiana's 126. Ohio has 125, Iowa 107, and Illinois 105.

That's not a one-window fluke. Wisconsin also led the 2020 to 2022 window with 125 entries, and it tops the all-time books with 1,822 entries through 2019. Boone and Crockett calls Buffalo County the epicenter.

**The fine print:** Record-book entries count the very top of the herd, not the average buck on your lease. A statewide rank says nothing about your county, and the record book leans hard on one corner of the state. If you want to know why ag-country bucks can look older than they are, see [our Field Note on that](/blog/why-ag-country-bucks-look-older-than-they-are/).

## Deer Numbers: B

**How we graded it:** The latest season's harvest against recent seasons. Above average and in the top ten seasons ever is an A. Within 10 percent below average is a B. Ten to 25 percent below is a C. Anything worse is a D. A season that's above average but not top ten lands in the B range.

Wisconsin hunters had registered 339,481 deer across all seasons as of Jan. 20, 2026, and DNR had not yet called the total final. DNR told the Natural Resources Board that compares with 317,976 in 2024, which makes 2025 about 7 percent higher. The 2023 total was 294,024. DNR's acting deer program specialist said 2025 had the highest buck harvest since 2007 and the highest overall harvest since 2012.

We compared 2025 to the recent seasons DNR has confirmed and didn't build a three-year average like we did for Minnesota. Reports of 2022's total conflict, so we left that year out.

It's a good year, not a record. Wisconsin's all-time high is 618,374 deer in 2000, so 2025 is about 45 percent below it.

One season didn't follow the trend. DNR says every season ran well above its five-year average except the nine-day gun season. That season registered a preliminary 182,084 deer, down 0.8 percent from 2024.

**The fine print:** Reported harvest is a floor, not a count of every deer on the ground, and the statewide number blends the northern forest with farm country. It also includes archery, which is where a lot of the growth came from. Check the county numbers for the ground you want.

## Access: B

**How we graded it:** Public land as a share of the state's total land. Twenty percent or more is an A, 10 to 19 percent is a B, 5 to 9 percent is a C, and under 5 percent is a D.

Wisconsin lands in the B range. The state's public land comes to roughly 5.9 million acres out of about 35 million, which works out to about 17 percent. DNR's own materials say the state has over 6 million acres of public land, which gives about the same answer.

**The fine print:** This is a statewide grade, and the big public acreage sits in the north, which is forest, not corn and soybean country. We didn't find a public-land share for the southern and western farm counties alone. If we had graded only the farm counties, we expect this grade would be lower.

DNR also lists about 8,000 acres of Voluntary Public Access land, where private owners let the public hunt, but that's a small slice next to the 6 million public acres.

That's what makes a lease worth looking at in Wisconsin. In the farm counties, most of the ground you can hunt is ground somebody owns.

## Herd Health: C

**How we graded it:** CWD and EHD results. An A is no wild CWD and no big EHD losses. A B means CWD is present but confined to a few counties and shows up in about 1 percent or less of tested deer, with a minus for a regional disease hit. A C means CWD is widespread or a region loses deer to EHD year after year. A D is both.

**CWD:** Wild deer have now tested positive in about 51 of Wisconsin's 72 counties, and DNR's broader "CWD-affected" list, which includes counties near a detection, is longer. In 2025 testing, 2,006 of 17,759 deer came back positive, about 11 percent. Most of those, 87 percent, were in the Southern Farmland Zone, where the rate ran around 23 percent. That's widespread by any measure, and it's why this category is a C.

**EHD:** DNR has logged more than 1,600 reports of dead deer in southeast Wisconsin this fall, the largest EHD outbreak the state has recorded. DNR says it isn't cutting harvest opportunities because of it. EHD has turned up in five of the last seven years, but the earlier outbreaks were small and scattered, so we didn't count it as a region losing deer year after year. That's why this stays a C and not a D.

**The fine print:** Testing isn't random, and heavy sampling in the hot spots pulls the percentage up. It tells you how common CWD is among the deer tested, not among the deer on your lease. For the basics, read [our CWD explainer](/blog/chronic-wasting-disease-explained/) and check DNR's CWD map for the county you want.

## Lease Market and Law: B-minus

**How we graded it:** Two halves. One is Wisconsin's landowner liability law, and whether it still protects a landowner who accepts a lease payment. The other is cropland cost against the national average, since land cost pushes lease prices up. Our grade averages the two halves. On cropland, within about 15 percent of the national average is an A, 15 to 30 percent over is a B, 30 to 50 percent over is a C, and more than 50 percent over is a D.

**The law stops at $2,000 a year (law half: C+).** Wisconsin's recreational immunity law is Wis. Stat. 895.52. It says a private landowner isn't liable for injuries to people using the land for recreation, which includes hunting. Think of it as a $2,000 tripwire. The protection doesn't apply if the owner collects money, goods, or services for the use and the total payments received that year go over $2,000.

Some things don't count as payment. A gift of wild animals or other product from the activity is one. A donation for managing the property is another, and so are payments from a government body or a nonprofit. The immunity also doesn't cover a malicious act or a malicious failure to warn of a known hazard.

The line comes up fast. Eighty acres at $25 an acre is exactly $2,000, and a 160-acre farm at the same rate is $4,000. That's our example, not a market price. The shield is better than Minnesota's, which drops once a hunter pays anything, but a typical lease can still cross it. That's our reading of the statute, not legal advice. We read a summary of the statute, so check the current text before you sign anything.

**Cropland is costly, but not Iowa-costly (cropland half: B).** USDA's 2026 land values report puts Wisconsin cropland at $7,600 per acre. The U.S. average is $6,020, so Wisconsin runs about 26 percent higher. For comparison, Iowa is at $10,700, Illinois $10,200, Ohio $10,100, Indiana $8,600, Minnesota $7,300, and Michigan $6,600.

A C-plus for the law and a B for cropland average to 2.65, which rounds to a B-minus.

**The fine print:** We couldn't find an official Wisconsin per-acre hunting lease price. One leasing marketplace, HLRBO, has Wisconsin listings that run up to about $53 per acre. That's a marketplace's asking price, not a survey. For the ranges we've found across the Corn Belt, see [what hunters actually pay to lease ground](/blog/cost-of-leasing-ag-ground/). Get anything you sign reviewed by a lawyer.

## What the Grades Mean If You Lease in Wisconsin

- **Know the $2,000 line.** If the owner's total hunting income for the year goes over it, the law's shield stops. Expect a written release and proof of liability insurance, and see [what to look for when leasing hunting land](/blog/leasing-hunting-property-what-to-look-for/).
- **Don't pay Buffalo County prices for bucks you can't verify.** Wisconsin's record-book lead is real, but it leans hard on one corner of the state. Ask what's actually been taken off the farm you're looking at.
- **Check the county for baiting and CWD rules.** Baiting bans cover many counties and renew with each new positive, so check before you plan a feed plot or a bait pile.
- **Plan on private ground in farm country.** The 17 percent is real, but most of the big public acreage is up north.
- **Check the town before you buy a rifle.** State rules allow rifles, but local ordinances can add limits.
- **Check the dates and the license costs.** Our [Wisconsin deer season dates page](/wisconsin-deer-season-dates/) has this year's seasons, and our [7-state license guide](/blog/deer-hunting-license-guide-seven-state/) has the costs.

## FAQ: Wisconsin Deer Hunting Leases

### Is Wisconsin a good state to lease deer ground?

For bucks, yes. Wisconsin is first in Boone and Crockett's 2022 to 2024 entries, and the 2025 harvest was the biggest in 13 years. About 17 percent of the state is public land, most of it in the north. CWD is widespread, and the state's liability law stops protecting a landowner once lease payments pass $2,000 a year.

### How much does it cost to lease hunting land in Wisconsin?

We couldn't find an official Wisconsin per-acre lease price. One leasing marketplace, HLRBO, has Wisconsin listings that run up to about $53 per acre. That's an asking price, not a survey. USDA puts Wisconsin cropland at $7,600 per acre, about 26 percent above the national average, and land cost tends to pull lease prices up.

### Is a landowner protected from liability if they lease land to hunters in Wisconsin?

Partly. Wis. Stat. 895.52 protects a private landowner who allows recreation, but the protection doesn't apply if the owner's total payments for the use go over $2,000 in the year. Gifts of game and payments from governments or nonprofits don't count toward the total. A written lease, a signed release, and insurance are the usual safeguards. Talk to a lawyer before signing.

### How many deer can I take on a Wisconsin lease?

Wisconsin has no one-buck rule. The bag limit is one buck per unused buck authorization, and the authorizations are specific to a season and weapon. Antlerless deer need an antlerless authorization that's valid for a specific zone, unit, or land type. Check the rules for the farm's county before you lease.

## The Bottom Line

Wisconsin gets a B-minus (2.9). That's level with Michigan's 2.9 and ahead of Ohio's 2.8, Indiana's C-plus (2.5), and Iowa's C (2.0), and it sits just behind Minnesota's B (3.0). It has the best buck grade in the series, and the Herd Health C is what holds it back. Read the Access B carefully too: it's a statewide number, and most of that public ground is in the north woods.

If you lease in Wisconsin, ask about the CWD history and the county's baiting rules before the scouting. Get the release and the insurance in writing, keep an eye on the $2,000 line, and don't count on public ground near the farm.
