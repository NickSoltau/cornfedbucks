Every shotgun hunter in a straight-wall cartridge (SWC) state eventually has the same conversation with himself: **is it time to switch from a shotgun slug to a straight-wall rifle**, or just keep running what he's got? We've covered the [smoothbore-vs-rifled barrel decision](/blog/smoothbore-vs-rifled-shotgun-barrels) and [which gauge fits which hunter](/blog/gauge-comparison-12ga-20ga-410) — both assume you're staying with a shotgun. This piece is about the bigger decision underneath those.

This isn't a "rifles are better" article. A rifled 12-gauge with the right sabot slug is still a legitimate 100–150 yard deer gun. If that's covering your shots, there's no performance reason to spend money changing anything.

The switch only makes sense once you look honestly at three things: recoil, realistic range, and what it actually costs you to convert.

## Recoil: The Gap Between Slugs and Straight-Wall Rifles Is Real

This is usually the deciding factor, whether hunters admit it or not.

A full-power 12-gauge slug load carries felt recoil that [rivals or exceeds a .300 Win Mag](/blog/gauge-comparison-12ga-20ga-410) — enough to cause flinching in a lot of shooters, which costs more accuracy than the extra energy ever buys back. A 20-gauge softens that considerably, but it's still a stouter kick than most SWC rifles.

If recoil is what's actually driving the question, the honest answer is usually yes — a .350 Legend in a comparable-weight gun is a different shooting experience entirely, not just a milder version of the same one.

## Range: Straight-Wall Rifles Win the Distance Argument

A rifled sabot slug is rated by most manufacturers for solid hits out to 150–200 yards, but that's the ceiling with premium ammo and a well-fitted barrel — not the average.

A straight-wall rifle holds meaningfully more energy at that same distance. The [.400 Legend still carries roughly 1,100+ ft-lbs past 200 yards](/blog/350-legend-vs-400-legend), well past what a slug is doing at that range.

For most row-crop hunters, this matters less than it sounds like it should. If your shots run under 100 yards — a lot of timber and funnel setups do — a shotgun slug is already plenty of gun, and the rifle's extra range is capability you're not using.

If you're watching a full bean field or a long fence line where 150–200 yard shots are a real possibility, that's where the rifle's advantage actually shows up on paper.

## Cost: Where the Math Gets Personal

This depends entirely on what you already own. It's worth being honest with yourself rather than assuming either direction is automatically cheaper.

- **Already own a shotgun and a rifled barrel?** You're set up for slugs. Switching to a rifle means buying a whole new platform — rifle, scope, rings, and a break-in box of ammo — against a shotgun setup you've already paid for.
- **Still shooting a smoothbore with Foster slugs?** You're already looking at $100–$500 for a rifled barrel upgrade just to get to 150 yards. At that point, pricing a rifle setup against that upgrade cost — not against a shotgun you already own outright — is the fairer comparison.
- **Ongoing ammo cost:** Premium sabot slugs generally run more per round than factory rifle ammo in common SWC calibers, but exact pricing shifts enough that we're not putting a hard number on it here. Check current retail pricing before treating this as a deciding factor.

## Your Existing Gear Isn't Wasted Either Way

A shotgun you already own doesn't become dead weight if you add a rifle. Plenty of Midwest hunters run both — a shotgun for shotgun-only zones or a backup gun, and a rifle for their main SWC-legal ground.

For most people, this isn't an either/or purchase so much as a "what do I reach for on opening morning" decision. If you're leaning toward adding a rifle rather than replacing the shotgun outright, our [guide to choosing a straight-wall cartridge](/blog/how-to-choose-straight-wall-cartridge) walks through picking the right one for your platform and shot distances. A compact bolt-action like the Ruger American Gen II Ranch is a common entry point for hunters making this move for the first time.

## A Note on Indiana and Michigan's Rule Changes

Worth addressing directly, since it changes the "why bother with a straight-wall rifle at all" question in two of our core four states.

**Indiana** changed its rifle rules last year — Senate Enrolled Act 280, effective for hunting seasons beginning after June 30, 2025, allows centerfire rifles with a bullet diameter of .219" (5.56mm) or larger on both public and private land, removing the old cartridge case-length restrictions entirely. That's a much broader window than the straight-wall-only rule it replaced.

**Michigan** followed this year: on May 13, 2026, the Natural Resources Commission eliminated the Limited Firearm Deer Zone — the "rifle line" that used to restrict Lower Peninsula hunters to straight-wall cartridges only. Traditional centerfire rifles are now legal there too, for the 2026 season on.

In both states, straight-wall cartridges didn't become illegal — they're just no longer the only rifle option. So why does this site keep covering SWC gear and technique? Because a lot of hunters already own a .350 Legend, .400 Legend, or similar, and that rifle isn't going anywhere just because the law loosened. The investment — the rifle itself, the optic zeroed to it, the ammo they've already sighted in with — is the reason SWC content stays relevant here even where it's no longer legally required.

This isn't a "these states still make you buy a straight-wall rifle" article. It's a "this is still what a lot of hunters are actually running" one.

As always: **confirm your specific state, zone, and season against the current-year DNR digest before hunting.** Rules have moved twice in two years across these four states, and they're not done moving. See our [state-by-state SWC regulations guide](/blog/straight-wall-cartridge-state-regulations) for the fuller breakdown.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is a straight-wall rifle actually more accurate than a shotgun slug?
Not inherently — a well-fitted rifled barrel with the right sabot slug is plenty accurate inside its effective range. The rifle's advantage is holding energy and a flatter trajectory farther out, not raw accuracy at typical row-crop distances.

### Should I switch if my shots are all under 100 yards?
Probably not for range reasons alone. Inside 100 yards, a shotgun slug does the job. Recoil is the more common reason hunters switch even at short range.

### Do Indiana and Michigan's rule changes mean straight-wall rifles are obsolete there?
No. Both states expanded what's legal beyond straight-wall cartridges — they didn't remove straight-wall cartridges as an option. Hunters who already own an SWC rifle can keep using it; hunters starting fresh in those two states now have more rifle options to choose from.

## The Bottom Line

If recoil is the problem, switching to a straight-wall rifle solves it more completely than dropping a gauge does. If range past 100 yards is the problem, a rifle carries more energy there than even a good sabot slug out of a rifled barrel. And in Indiana and Michigan specifically, the rule changes give you more rifle options — they don't retire the ones you've already got.

If you already own a rifled shotgun and your shots stay inside 100 yards, there's no performance case for spending the money. The switch is about what you're actually hunting, not which platform is objectively "better."
