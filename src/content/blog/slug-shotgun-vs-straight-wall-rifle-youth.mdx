Getting a kid ready for their first deer season, and stuck between a **slug shotgun and a straight-wall rifle**? There's no single right answer here. But there is a right answer for your kid — and it usually comes down to recoil, not caliber charts.

## Why Recoil Matters More Than Power

A hard-kicking gun doesn't just hurt. It teaches a bad habit. A kid who gets punished by recoil starts flinching before they even pull the trigger. Once that flinch shows up, accuracy goes out the window — and so does confidence.

So the real question isn't "what hits hardest." It's this: what can your kid shoot 20 times in a row without dreading it?

## The Shotgun Side: 20-Gauge with Slugs

A youth-model 20-gauge is the traditional pick, and for good reason. It's familiar, it's affordable, and slug ammo is easy to find almost anywhere.

But it still kicks. A 20-gauge slug isn't a light load — smaller-framed shooters feel it plenty. Range stays short, too. Most slug guns are solid inside 75–100 yards, less if the barrel isn't rifled. We cover that gap in our [smoothbore vs. rifled barrel breakdown](/blog/smoothbore-vs-rifled-shotgun-barrels).

## The Rifle Side: .350 Legend or .400 Legend

For a young or new shooter, this really comes down to two calibers: .350 Legend or .400 Legend. Either one solves the recoil problem well. Felt recoil is noticeably milder than a 20-gauge slug — closer to a stiff .243 than a shotgun.

That's it, though. Don't go bigger.

Yes, somebody's kid is out there shooting a .450 Bushmaster with a big grin and no complaints. Good for them. But the whole point of moving a young hunter to a straight-wall rifle is to get *away* from punishing recoil — not just trade one hard-kicking gun for another. A .450 Bushmaster kicks harder than most 20-gauge slugs. Stepping up to it defeats the purpose.

This isn't about what a kid can tolerate. It's about building good habits — a steady trigger pull, no flinch, no dread walking up to the bench. That's a lot easier to teach on a .350 or .400 Legend than on anything bigger, no matter how tough the kid is.

Range stretches out too. A straight-wall rifle in either caliber holds accuracy out past 150–200 yards in the right hands — well beyond where a slug gun starts to fall apart. On ag-land ground with long sightlines across cut beans or standing corn, that extra range can matter.

The catch: cost. A dedicated youth rifle setup — rifle, optic, ammo — usually runs more upfront than adding a slug barrel to a shotgun the family already owns.

## What Actually Decides It

Skip the spec sheet. Walk through these instead:

- **Does your kid already flinch on a 20-gauge?** If yes, the rifle is probably the better move.
- **What's your family's realistic shot distance?** Tight ground blinds in timber favor the shotgun. Open ag-land favors the rifle.
- **Do you already own a shotgun?** A rifled slug barrel swap is cheaper than a whole new rifle setup.
- **Will they grow into it?** A youth-stocked rifle with an adjustable length-of-pull often has a longer useful life than a fixed-stock youth shotgun.

If you're still not sure, take them to a range with both — a rental or a buddy's gun works fine — and just watch. The gun they don't flinch on is the gun that's going to put meat in the freezer.
