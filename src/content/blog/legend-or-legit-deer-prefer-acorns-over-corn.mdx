Come October, one line shows up at every deer camp: "The acorns are dropping. The deer won't touch the corn or beans now." If you hunt row-crop ground, that's a big claim. It says a handful of oak trees can beat a whole field of food. So, do deer prefer acorns over corn? Mostly, with some fine print.

## The Claim

Deer like acorns more than anything else. When the oaks start dropping, they leave the ag fields and live under the oak trees until the nuts run out.

## Do Deer Prefer Acorns Over Corn? Verdict: Legit, With Fine Print

The first half of the claim holds up. Acorns are a top fall food, and deer change their behavior when they fall.

The best deer study we found is a [1993 paper by William McShea and Georg Schwede](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/33017) in the *Journal of Mammalogy*. They tracked radio-collared does, 10 to 17 a year, in a Virginia forest from 1986 to 1989. In good acorn years, the does stretched their home ranges to take in the oak areas. At the peak of the drop, acorns made up about half of their feeding time.

The does also ate 70 percent of the marked acorns the researchers set out. That tells you how fast a good crop can disappear.

A [2026 study from Poland](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13403050/) came at it from the other side. Researchers Maria Bogdańska, Valentin Journé, and Michał Bogdziewicz looked at 19 hunting seasons of crop damage. Damage from red deer and wild boar was roughly 30 to 37 percent lower in the best oak seed years than in the worst. The authors think the animals ate acorns instead of crops, though the data can't prove that.

Think of a full fridge and a pizza delivery. You're not hungry. The groceries are right there. But when the pizza shows up, you eat the pizza first. On ag ground, corn and beans are the groceries and acorns are the pizza.

Which acorns matter? The [Illinois Natural History Survey](https://hunt.inhs.illinois.edu/hunting-trapping/deer-hunting/deer-ecology/) says white oak acorns are the preferred food, and red oak acorns fill the gap in years when white oaks produce poorly. Red oak acorns are more bitter.

## The Fine Print

- **"Pulled toward" isn't "gone from the field."** In the Polish data, big acorn years cut crop damage by about a third. They didn't cut it to zero. Nothing we found shows deer walking away from crops altogether.
- **No oaks, no pull.** On a lot of Midwest ground, the only oaks are in a woodlot, a creek bottom, or along a fence line. If there aren't oaks within reach, there's nothing to pull deer away from the field.
- **Acorn crops swing from year to year.** The McShea paper is literally titled "Variable Acorn Crops." In its one poor acorn year, the does didn't stretch their ranges at all. A heavy drop last year says little about this one.
- **We didn't find a study that measured this directly on Midwest row-crop ground.** The Virginia work was in a forest, on does. The Polish work was red deer and wild boar, not whitetails. That makes this a strong lead, not gospel.

This also doesn't clash with our rule that food isn't the limiting factor on ag ground. See [Food Plots Are Optional. Sanctuary Acres Aren't.](/blog/sanctuary-acres/) for that one. Deer on corn-and-bean country aren't short on food. They just have a favorite.

## What to Do With This

- **Walk your oaks now.** Look for acorns on the ground, cracked shells, and fresh tracks and droppings under the trees. Quick ID: white oak leaves have rounded lobes, and red oak leaves have bristle-tipped points (see the leaf comparison above). Not sure deer are using them? A trail camera on the best oak will tell you. See [Trail Camera Strategy for Pressured Row-Crop Ground](/blog/trail-camera-cell-cam-strategy/).
- **Look at how the oaks connect to your fields.** If oaks sit between bedding and a crop edge, that's a travel route worth a closer look. See [Terrain Funnels in Row-Crop Country](/blog/terrain-funnels-row-crop-country/), and check the wind first, same as always with a [preseason wind chart](/blog/building-a-preseason-wind-chart/).
- **Don't write off your crop-edge stands.** If your ground has no acorn crop this year, the field edge is still your best bet. See [reading a freshly cut soybean field](/blog/reading-a-cut-soybean-field/) and [standing vs. cut corn](/blog/standing-vs-cut-corn-rifle-hunting/).
- **Leave the oaks standing.** If you're hinge cutting for cover, cut something else. See [Hinge-Cutting Fence Lines for Deer Bedding Cover](/blog/hinge-cutting-fence-lines-bedding-cover/).

## FAQ: Acorns, Corn, and Deer Movement

### Do deer prefer acorns over corn?

Often, yes, in good acorn years. Research shows deer shift their feeding and home ranges toward oaks when acorns drop, and crop damage fell in big oak years in one large European study. How much it matters depends on whether oaks are within reach of your ground.

### Which acorns do deer like best?

White oak acorns. The Illinois Natural History Survey lists them as the preferred acorn, with more bitter red oak acorns filling the gap in years when white oaks produce poorly.

### Do deer stop eating corn and soybeans when acorns drop?

Not completely. In a 2026 Polish study, big oak crops cut crop damage by roughly 30 to 37 percent, not to zero. That study looked at red deer and wild boar, not whitetails.

### Should I hunt oaks or crop fields in October?

Scout the oaks first. If you find fresh sign under oak trees with acorns on the ground, they deserve a sit. If there's no acorn crop or no oaks in reach, stick with your crop edges.

## The Bottom Line

Call it Legit, with fine print. Acorns are a real pull, but they're a detour, not a replacement for the field. Find your oaks, check the crop, and keep your field-edge plan as the backup.
